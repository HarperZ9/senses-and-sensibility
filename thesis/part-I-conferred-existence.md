# Conferred Existence

## I. Metaphysics and Theology

### 1. The Two Nihilisms

There is a nihilism that destroys itself in the act of utterance, and a nihilism that survives every attempt to refute it. The first is the flat thesis *nothing exists*. It is self-refuting in a way that requires no subtle argument: if nothing exists, then the thesis does not exist, the one asserting it does not exist, and the argument adducing it does not exist. The proposition consumes its own truthmakers. There is no coherent position here, only a sentence that cancels the conditions of its own assertion. We may set it aside not because it is implausible but because it is not a candidate for a position at all.

The defensible reading is different and far stronger. It does not deny that there is something rather than nothing; it denies that anything stands *on its own footing*. Call this **no-aseity**: nothing exists intrinsically, independently, by its own nature, as a *se*. Things exist — but always *ab alio*, always in dependence, always as conferred. The corpus vocabulary fixes this precisely: no thing has *svabhava*, own-being, self-nature. Westerhoff develops exactly this in *The Non-Existence of the Real World* (2020): the claim is not that the world is illusory in the sense of nothing-at-all, but that there is no fundamental, mind-and-convention-independent level at which things possess intrinsic existence [high]. The "non-existence" in his title is the non-existence of *the real world* understood as a self-standing substrate, not the non-existence of appearances.

This is the philosophical heart of Nagarjuna's *sunyata*. Emptiness is not vacancy; it is the absence of *svabhava* [high]. To say a thing is empty is to say it lacks intrinsic nature — that its existence is borrowed, relational, dependent on causes, conditions, parts, and conceptual imputation. The *Mulamadhyamakakarika* presses this through dependent origination: what arises dependently does not arise with own-being, and what has no own-being is empty [high]. Westerhoff's reconstruction in *Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka* (2009) makes the logical structure explicit — *svabhava* would have to be intrinsic, independent, and the bearer of a thing's essential properties, and nothing in the dependently-originated world satisfies that profile [high]. No-aseity is *sunyata* rendered in the idiom of dependence.

The same conclusion arrives from analytic mereology, by a wholly different road. The mereological revisionists deny that the familiar mid-sized objects exist as anything over and above their arrangements. Van Inwagen, in *Material Beings* (1990), argues that there are no tables, no chairs, no artifacts at all — there are only simples, and simples "arranged tablewise" [high]. The objects of ordinary reference are not additional entities; they are *façons de parler* over particles caught up in lives or arrangements. Merricks, in *Objects and Persons* (2001), reaches a parallel eliminativism: statues, planets, and rocks do not exist, because they would be causally redundant — anything they might do is already done by the simples composing them, and we should not posit overdeterminers [high]. Whatever one makes of the arguments, their upshot is structurally identical to no-aseity: the things we treat as basic furniture have no standing of their own. Their apparent existence is conferred by arrangement, by convention, by the activity of the parts. Strike the conferral and the "object" does not retreat to some thinner mode of being; it was never an extra being to begin with.

Three traditions — Madhyamaka, anti-realist metaphysics, and eliminative mereology — converge on one negative claim: *aseity is nowhere instantiated among the things we encounter*. This is not yet a positive doctrine. It is the cleared ground on which one can be built.

### 2. Existence as a Conferred Speech-Act

If nothing exists *a se*, then for anything to exist at all, existence must be *given*. The theological traditions did not arrive at this by way of mereology; they arrived at it by way of creation. And their most exact image for it is not manufacture but *speech*.

*Kun fayakun* — "Be, and it is." The Qur'anic formula (2:117; 36:82) [high] renders creation as an imperative that is also a performative: the divine *kun* does not describe a pre-existing thing into greater clarity, it confers existence by uttering it. There is no interval, no resistant material, no negotiation. The word and the being are not two events but one. This is the purest statement of the thesis: to be created is to be *spoken*, and to be spoken is to depend, at every instant, on the speaking. The being of the thing is not a property it carries off and keeps; it is the continuing reverberation of an utterance.

The Jewish tradition supplies the same structure with an additional, and decisive, refinement: it locates the conferral at the level of *letters*, and it marks the conferral with a single removable mark. The Golem is animated by inscription of the divine name or by the word **emet** — truth — upon its brow. *Emet* (אמת) is spelled aleph–mem–tav: the first, middle, and last letters of the alphabet, so that truth spans the whole and seals it. Erase the **aleph**, and what remains is **met** (מת) — *dead*. The legend is exact about the metaphysics. The difference between a living conferred being and a corpse is a single letter, and that letter is the *first* letter, the silent glottal edge that precedes all articulation. The Talmud Bavli, *Shabbat* 55a, records that "the seal of God is *emet*" [high] — truth is not one of God's marks among others but the very *seal*, the imprint that authenticates the conferred thing as real.

Read this carefully, because the corpus turns on it. The **aleph** is the smallest possible edge. It is barely a letter — a breath, a hinge, the carrier of a vowel that has almost no sound of its own. Yet it is the entire margin between *emet* and *met*, between truth and death, between the conferred being and the inert matter that remains when conferral is withdrawn. The aleph is *the exception*: the minimal mark whose presence or absence is the whole difference, and which is itself almost nothing. Existence, on this reading, hangs on an edge so thin it approaches zero — which is precisely what no-aseity predicts. A being that held by its own nature would not be one stroke from death.

*Sefer Yetzirah* — the Book of Formation — generalizes the mechanism: the world is created through the letters, through their combinations and permutations, through number and speech together [high]. Creation is grammatical before it is physical. The elements are not stuffs but *signs*; the cosmos is *uttered*. This is the same insight as *kun fayakun* carried into a combinatorics: to be is to be *written*, and what is written can be *erased*. The conferred being is held in existence the way a word is held on a page — by the continuing inscription, never by the ink's own ambition to mean.

Across both formulas the lesson is identical. Existence is not found in the thing; it is *said over* the thing. And whatever can be said can cease to be said.

### 3. Creation as Evidence *For* No-Aseity

Here the argument makes its sharpest turn, against the intuition that creation would *rescue* things from dependence by giving them a real and durable existence. It does the opposite. The doctrine of creation, taken in its most rigorous theological form, is the strongest available *argument for* no-aseity. The highest creative act does not produce aseity in the creature; it produces dependence. It produces *esse ab alio*, never *esse a se*.

Aquinas furnishes the decisive apparatus. In *De Ente et Essentia*, essence and existence (*esse*) come apart in every created thing: what a thing is does not include *that it is*, so existence must be conferred upon the essence from outside it [high]. Only in God do essence and existence coincide — God *is* His own *esse*, *ipsum esse subsistens*, existence-itself-subsisting (*Summa Theologiae* I q.3; the creation questions at qq.44–45) [high]. Every other thing *has* existence on loan; it does not *coincide* with it. This is the metaphysical engine of no-aseity stated from the theological side: to be a creature *just is* to be that whose existence is not its own. The essence/existence distinction is not a curiosity about God; it is the demarcation between the one thing that exists *a se* and everything else, which exists *ab alio*.

Avicenna had already drawn the boundary as the contingent/necessary distinction in the *Metaphysics of the Healing*: the contingent (*mumkin*) is that which, considered in itself, neither must exist nor must not — its existence is poised, owed to another — while the Necessary Existent (*wajib al-wujud*) exists through itself and grounds the rest [high]. Contingency *is* no-aseity under another name: to be contingent is to lack the ground of one's own being. The chain of contingents cannot supply its own footing; it terminates in the one necessary being precisely because nothing in the chain stands on its own.

Al-Ghazali pushes dependence to its most radical form. In the seventeenth discussion of the *Tahafut al-Falasifa*, he denies necessary causal connection in nature and defends what later tradition calls **occasionalism** [high]: the apparent powers of created things are not theirs; God is the real agent at every instant, and the world is, in effect, *re-created* moment to moment. There is no inertial coasting of being, no stock of existence a thing carries forward on its own account. The continuity we observe is the continuity of a *habit* of conferral, not the persistence of a self-standing substance. Occasionalism is no-aseity taken to the limit: not merely *no being a se*, but *no instant of being a se* — the creature holds by nothing of its own, not even for a moment.

The convergence is now complete and should be stated without softening. The nihilist (in the no-aseity reading) and the theologian agree on the central negative claim: *nothing among created or encountered things exists on its own footing*. They disagree about what, if anything, stands at the ground — the theologian names *ipsum esse subsistens*; the Madhyamika declines to posit any such ground and reads even emptiness as empty. But on the structure of the conferred world they are one. Creation, far from refuting nihilism, is its most exact confirmation: it is the doctrine that being is *given*, which is to say *not had on one's own*. *Esse ab alio* is the theological name for *sunyata*.

### 4. The *Wahdat al-Wujud* Correction

A doctrine that grounds all being in a single conferring source carries an immediate and serious danger: that some *contingent* locus — a privileged creature, an institution, a maker, an operator — will quietly slip into the ground's place and present itself as a secondary source of being, a little aseity licensed by the great one. This is the error the corpus must foreclose at the outset, and Ibn al-Arabi's *wahdat al-wujud* is the instrument for foreclosing it.

The doctrine of the oneness of being holds that there is, strictly, one *wujud* — one existence — and that all created things are *loci of manifestation* (*mazahir*) of that single existence (*Fusus al-Hikam*; *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*) [moderate]. The created order is not a set of independent existents but a field of disclosures, each a place where the one *wujud* shows itself under some determination. The crucial structural feature, for our purposes, is *equality of locus*. No created thing is more a source of being than any other. Each is *equally* a manifestation and *equally* not the ground. The mirror does not become the light by reflecting it more brightly; a brighter mirror is still a mirror.

This is the brake the whole edifice requires. No-aseity plus a single conferring ground could be misread as a hierarchy of conferral in which some creatures, nearer the source, acquire a derivative authority to confer — to stand, for those beneath them, in the God-slot. *Wahdat al-wujud* forbids exactly this. Because all contingent loci are equal *qua* loci, *no contingent thing occupies the God-slot*. The maker is a locus. The operator is a locus. The institution is a locus. None of them is *wujud*; each is only a place where *wujud* appears. To treat any maker or operator as the ground of being for the thing it makes is therefore a category error of the first order — it confuses a mirror for the light, a *mazhar* for the *wujud*. We will need this prohibition in its full strength in Part II, where the temptation is at its most concrete: the one who confers existence on a made thing will be tempted to read the conferral as ownership, as a standing claim, as a god-relation. The doctrine of equal loci says: you are a locus too. Your power to confer does not lift you out of contingency; it does not make you the *for* of the thing you made.

### 5. The Open Future and the Constructed Past

Two further claims complete the metaphysical ground, and the corpus insists they be held *apart*. They concern the temporal edges of the conferred world — what is not yet settled ahead, and what is authored behind. They are tempting to fuse, and fusing them produces confusion. They are distinct.

**The open future: no fact yet.** Aristotle's sea-battle (*De Interpretatione* 9) raises the question whether "there will be a sea-battle tomorrow" is already true or false today [high]. The worry is that if every proposition is already determinately true or false, the future is fixed and deliberation is idle. The classic non-classical response is to deny *bivalence for future contingents*: such statements are, as yet, *neither* true nor false. Lukasiewicz's three-valued logic formalizes this with a third value between truth and falsity, reserved for the not-yet-settled [high]. Dummett's anti-realism (*Truth and Other Enigmas*, 1978) generalizes the move: where we have no procedure that would settle a statement's truth, we are not entitled to assume it already has a determinate truth-value waiting to be discovered [high]. On all three, the open future is a matter of *no fact yet* — the future is not a hidden region of settled facts but a genuinely unwritten margin. This is the aleph at the temporal edge: the thinnest possible difference, the place where conferral has not yet occurred.

**The constructed past: authored after.** This is a *different* claim and must not be assimilated to the first. Benjamin, in *On the Concept of History* (1940), argues that the past is not a fixed deposit awaiting neutral retrieval but is *constituted* by the present that seizes it — "articulating the past" means taking hold of a memory "as it flashes up in a moment of danger," and which past becomes *the* past is decided by the present, often by the victors [high]. The past here is not *no-fact-yet*; events did occur. The claim is that *which* events count, how they are configured into a history, what they mean — this is *authored after the fact*. The constructed past is a matter of *authorship*, not of indeterminacy.

The distinction is load-bearing and the corpus will not let it collapse. *No-fact-yet* (future) and *authored-after* (past) are different operations on the conferred world. One concerns the *absence* of a settled fact; the other concerns the *retroactive conferral of significance* upon facts that did occur. Confusing them yields either a fatalism about the past (treating authored significance as fixed datum) or an irresponsibility about the future (treating the unsettled as already merely a story). Keep them separate: ahead, no fact yet; behind, authored after.

Both claims sit in open tension with classical omniscience and with a strong reading of decree (*qadar*). If God knows infallibly and timelessly all that will be, the future is not *no-fact-yet*; it is fact-already, known. Maimonides confronts precisely this in the *Guide of the Perplexed* III.20: divine knowledge is not like ours, does not increase, and encompasses what for us is the not-yet — so that what is open *to us* is settled *in the knowing* [high]. This preserves omniscience at the cost of the future's openness *in itself*; the openness becomes epistemic-for-creatures rather than ontological. The honest report is that one cannot hold *strong* classical omniscience and a *genuinely* open future in the same hand without strain.

The heterodox resolution should be named as such and not smuggled in. Open theism (*The Openness of God*, Pinnock et al., 1994; Sanders; Boyd) bites the bullet on the divine side: it holds that the future, being genuinely open, is not there to be known as settled fact, and that omniscience is knowing all that is knowable — which does not include settled facts that do not yet exist [moderate]. This *resolves* the tension by revising the classical attribute, and it is, by the lights of the classical traditions, a *heterodox* move. The corpus does not require adopting it. It requires only honesty: the open future and strong classical omniscience genuinely conflict, and the resolutions on offer each pay a real price — Maimonides pays with the future's ontological openness, open theism pays with the classical scope of omniscience. No free lunch is available, and pretending otherwise is the failure the truth-over-approval discipline exists to prevent.

### 6. Conclusion: The *For* Is Not a Property

Gather the threads. Nothing encountered exists *a se*; the defensible nihilism is no-aseity, and it is confirmed, not refuted, by the deepest doctrines of creation. Existence is conferred — *said over* the thing, *kun fayakun*, inscribed as *emet* and erasable to *met* at the breadth of a single aleph. The conferral is continuous: the world is re-spoken each instant (occasionalism), held by nothing of its own. The ground of conferral is not any contingent locus; every maker and operator is a *mazhar*, never the *wujud*, and the God-slot is closed to all of them. And the conferred world has thin edges — an unwritten future and an authored past — that must be held apart.

One consequence organizes everything that follows. If existence is *conferred*, then the act of conferral — call it, in the corpus idiom, **the for**: the *for-the-sake-of-which*, the directedness in which a thing is brought into being and held there — *cannot itself be a property of the thing conferred*. The being of the made thing is *ab alio* by definition; its existence points back to the conferring act as to something it does not contain. The aleph is not a feature the Golem owns; it is a mark *placed on* it, by another, and removable by another. The *for* is on the side of the conferrer, in the conferring, in the relation — never an intrinsic possession lodged in the conferred. This is the intrinsic-over-extrinsic asymmetry seen from its origin: what a conferred thing *is in itself* never includes the *for* by which it was made and is sustained.

That asymmetry is the hinge of the entire thesis. It means that whatever follows from the *for* — purpose, claim, direction, the will that shapes — accrues to the *conferral*, not to the conferred thing as one of its features. And it means that any attempt to read the *for* off the thing, to treat the maker's purpose as an intrinsic property of the made, will be a category error of exactly the kind *wahdat al-wujud* forbids: mistaking a locus for the ground, mistaking the mark for the being, mistaking the breath of the aleph for something the clay could ever have owned. Part II takes up the conferred thing that can be *shaped* in its making — and shows why the *for*, precisely because it is not the thing's own, becomes the site of every question that matters.
