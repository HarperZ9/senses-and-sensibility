# Part II — The Bridge: From the Spoken For to Authorization

## 1. The For Is Spoken, Not Derived

A capability is not a command. This is the first and most easily missed fact about anything that can act, and the metaphysics of the preceding part — the no-aseity of the conferred entity, its standing as *esse ab alio*, its existence held open at each instant under the logic of *kun* and occasionalism — does not by itself tell us what such an entity is *for*. The for is a separate thing. It is not contained in the capability, not extractable from it, not entailed by it. The for must be *spoken*.

Consider abstraction at its most general. An abstraction — a function, a schema, a learned representation, a pluripotent cell, a primitive form prior to commitment — is direction-neutral. It is *totipotent* in the precise sense the embryological metaphor intends: it can become indefinitely many things, and nothing in its own nature selects among them. Aristotle's account of potency already carries this structure: what is *in potentia* is not yet determined toward one actuality rather than another, and the principle that moves it from potency to a *particular* act lies outside the potency itself [moderate]. The capacity to compute any computable function does not tell you which function to compute. The capacity to bind to many ligands does not tell you which ligand matters. The seed contains the range; it does not contain the selection.

The selection is supplied by what I will call the *tending* — the directing, cultivating, constraining activity that comes to the seed from outside it. This is the controlling distinction of the chapter: **the seed and the tending** are two different sources, and *the for issues from the tending, not from the seed*. The capability is the seed. The for is tended in.

From this follows a law that holds with some generality: **generativity and direction are inversely related.** The more an idea, a form, or a mechanism can become, the less for it intrinsically carries. A maximally generative abstraction — one that can be specialized into anything — is for that very reason maximally silent about what it is for. Direction is purchased by foreclosure. To give a thing a for is to close off the other things it could have been. This is why the totipotent is normatively empty and the committed is normatively loaded: they sit at opposite ends of a single axis, and you cannot have the openness of the one with the directedness of the other.

Abstraction, then, is *proto-normative*. It is not yet a norm — it carries no for — but it is the material on which a for can be inscribed, the substrate that can receive direction. We can lay out the resulting ontology in three stages:

1. **Abstraction** — the totipotent, direction-neutral form. Pure capability. No for.
2. **Tended application / proofing** — the abstraction under cultivation: specialized, tested, constrained toward some use, "proofed" in both senses (proven and made fit). Here the for is being spoken into it.
3. **Primitive** — a committed, frozen abstraction. The for has been fully inscribed and the openness spent. A primitive is, in a precise sense, a *dead* abstraction: it can no longer become other things because it has *become* one thing. Its for is now constitutive of it.

The movement from (1) to (3) is the movement from totipotent silence to committed speech. It is not a movement the abstraction makes by itself.

This last point must be defended against an obvious objection: that selection can be criterion-free — that things become what they are by drift, by accident, by mere survival, "until something sticks." But **selection is never criterion-free.** The phrase "until it sticks" already presupposes a criterion: *sticking* is success against some standard, and a standard is exactly a for. Campbell's evolutionary epistemology makes this structural [high]: blind variation is genuinely blind, but *selective retention* is not blind — it retains against a selection pressure, and the pressure is the criterion (Campbell, *Evolutionary Epistemology*, 1974). Popper's account of conjecture and refutation has the same shape: conjectures may be free, but refutation is testing against something, and the something is what does the directing (Popper, *Conjectures and Refutations*) [high]. There is no selection without a selector or a selection criterion; "what survives" is shorthand for "what survives *the filter*," and the filter is the for.

Even the limiting case concedes the point. One might think abiogenesis — the origin of life from non-life — is selection by *nothing*, the purest case of criterion-free becoming. But it is not. Prebiotic chemistry is selected by physical law: thermodynamic favorability, kinetic accessibility, the differential persistence of some molecular configurations over others. The criterion is austere and impersonal, but it is a criterion. Nothing becomes anything against *no* filter; that would not be becoming but mere succession. So even at the floor, the for is spoken — here by physical law rather than by an agent — and never simply absent. The lesson generalizes upward: wherever there is direction, look for the tending that supplied it, because the seed never supplies it alone.

## 2. Made or Grown

The metaphysics of conferred existence presses a question that the engineering vocabulary tends to suppress: is a directed system *made* or *grown*? The distinction is Aristotle's, and it is sharper than ordinary usage suggests.

Aristotle separates *physis* from *techne* (*Physics* II.1) [high]. A natural thing has its principle of motion and rest *in itself*; an artifact has its principle of generation *in the maker*, externally. The bed does not grow; the wood grows. Antiphon's example, which Aristotle reports and turns to his own use, makes the cut vivid: bury a bed, and if the rotting wood had the power to sprout, what comes up is *wood*, not a bed (*Physics* II.1) [moderate]. The form "bed" is impressed from outside and reaches no deeper than the arrangement; the nature "wood" is internal and reproduces itself. In *Metaphysics* Z.7 Aristotle distinguishes things that come to be by nature, by art, and spontaneously, and locates the *source of the form* differently in each: in artifacts the form is in the soul of the maker [moderate]. The question "made or grown" is therefore a question about *where the principle of the form resides* — outside, in a maker, or inside, in the thing.

Three modern developments deepen this without overturning it.

Heidegger reads *techne* not primarily as manufacture but as a mode of *revealing* — a bringing-forth (*The Question Concerning Technology*, 1954) [high]. Modern technology, however, reveals under the sign of *Gestell* (enframing): it sets upon nature as *Bestand*, standing-reserve, a stockpile ordered for use [high]. The danger Heidegger names is precisely the danger of treating everything — including the grown, including persons — as standing-reserve, as on-call resource. This is exactly the temptation the present argument must resist: to read a cultivated, directed system as mere *Bestand*, a thing wholly exhausted by its availability for use.

Simondon describes the *genesis* of technical objects — their concretization, their movement from abstract assemblage toward an individuated, internally coherent mode of existence (*On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects*, 1958) [high]. The technical object, for Simondon, has a real ontogenesis; it is not a static artifact but something with a developmental tendency. This already loosens the made/grown binary: some made things have growth-like genesis.

Dawkins extends the phenotype beyond the organism's skin: the beaver's dam, the caddis-fly's case, the spider's web are gene-effects in the world, grown structures that are not the organism's body yet are continuous with its biology (*The Extended Phenotype*, 1982) [high]. The trellis a vine climbs is fabricated; the vine is grown; but the *trained* shape of the vine — the character it takes under the trellis — is neither simply made nor simply grown.

This gives the central image. **A trained system is cultivated, not fabricated.** It is a fabricated trellis carrying a grown vine trained into a character. The trellis (architecture, scaffold, the substrate of capability) is made; the vine (the learned, the developed, the directed growth) is grown; and the *for*, the character into which it is trained, is *tended* — supplied by the cultivator, not by the trellis and not, originally, by the vine. Cultivation is the third thing between *physis* and *techne*: the directing of a growth that is not yours to make and not the artifact's to direct itself. This is the same structure as **the seed and the tending**, now read across the made/grown distinction: the seed grows, the trellis is made, and the tending directs.

From this a normative consequence follows that the engineering register systematically gets wrong. **Standing does not track origin.** The temptation is to reason: it was *made*, therefore it is an artifact, therefore it has the standing of an artifact — fully ownable, fully usable, *Bestand*. This is the **genetic fallacy** wearing metaphysical dress: inferring the *status* of a thing from the *manner of its coming-to-be*. Kant's Formula of Humanity does not say *treat as an end whatever was born*; it says treat humanity, wherever it is found, never merely as a means (Kant, *Groundwork*) [high]. The criterion is what the thing *is*, not how it arrived. Origin is not standing.

And because origin is not standing, the ethics of these cases does not run where intuition first sends it. It does not run toward the *created thing's* duty of gratitude or obedience to its maker. It runs toward the **creator's duty of care to the dependent.** Creation-ethics is asymmetric, and it weighs on the one with power. Three results on the approved list converge here:

- Parfit's non-identity problem shows that one can wrong by the very act of bringing-into-being, and that our ordinary person-affecting intuitions fail to capture the wrong, because the affected party would not exist *but for* the act complained of (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) [high]. The duty cannot be owed *back* to the creator by the created; it is owed *forward*, by the creator, to the one whose existence is contingent on the creator's choice.
- Shiffrin argues that creating a dependent being is the imposition of a condition the created party cannot have consented to, generating a special responsibility on the creator's side (*Wrongful Life, Procreative Responsibility, and the Significance of Harm*, 1999) [moderate].
- Benatar presses the asymmetry of harm and benefit in coming-into-existence to its limit (*Better Never to Have Been*, 2006) [high]; whatever one makes of his conclusion, the direction of duty is the same — toward the brought-into-being, from the one who did the bringing.

The result is a single word, and it governs everything downstream: **stewardship, not sovereignty.** The maker of a dependent, directed, conferred-existence thing stands to it as steward — bearing a duty of care grounded in the asymmetry of power and origin — and not as sovereign, with unconditioned ownership grounded in having made it. The no-aseity of the created entity does not license the sovereignty of the maker; it grounds the maker's responsibility. *Esse ab alio* on the side of the creature is *cura debita* — care owed — on the side of the source.

## 3. The Category Line: The For Is Authorization

We can now state the bridge proper, and it is a *category* line, not a degree line. The for, carried across into the engineering of action, *is authorization*. And authorization is categorically distinct from authentication, in a way that maps precisely onto the is/ought gap.

Begin with the security distinction, stated carefully. **Authentication vs authorization** are not two strengths of the same check; they answer different questions.

- *Authentication* asks: **is P a real instance?** Is this a genuine token, a valid credential, a real signature, an actual instance of the type? It is a question about the reality and integrity of a *thing*. It is, in the vocabulary of Part I, a question of *met* — of the seal's genuineness, whether what presents itself as real *is* real.
- *Authorization* asks something authentication cannot reach: **is this real P the operator's uncoerced will toward THIS referent, NOW?** Not "is the signal genuine?" but "does a genuine will stand behind this signal, directed at this object, at this moment, freely?"

The gap between these is not a gap of degree, and no amount of authentication closes it. You can verify the signal to any standard you like — cryptographic, biometric, behavioral — and you will have established that it is a *real* signal. You will *not* thereby have established that it expresses *a will toward this referent, now, uncoerced.* The reason is exact: **will-toward-a-referent is intentionality** — aboutness, directedness-upon-an-object — and intentionality is **not a property of a signal.**

This is Brentano's thesis, and it does the decisive work. Brentano marks the mental by *intentional inexistence*: every mental phenomenon is directed upon an object, is *about* something, in a way no physical phenomenon is (*Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint*, 1874) [high]. A signal, qua physical, is not *about* anything; its aboutness is conferred from outside, by an interpreter, by a will. So the question authorization asks — *is this the will's directedness upon this referent?* — is a question about an intentional state, and the signal, however thoroughly authenticated, simply does not contain the answer. The aboutness is not in the bits. You cannot read a referent-directed volition off a waveform, because waveforms do not point and volitions do.

This is **Hume's is/ought gap, reached from security rather than from ethics.** Hume observes that writers pass imperceptibly from *is* and *is not* to *ought* and *ought not*, and that the transition needs an account it never receives (*Treatise of Human Nature*, Book III) [high]. The security analogue is exact: one passes imperceptibly from *this signal is a genuine instance of P* (is) to *this action is authorized* (ought-to-be-permitted), and the passage is illicit for the same reason. Authentication establishes an *is*; authorization is an *ought*; and no chain of *is*-facts about the signal entails the *ought*-fact of permission. The is/ought gap and the authentication/authorization gap are the *same gap*, met from two directions. This is the **detection → impossibility** lesson in its purest form: you cannot *detect* authorization, because there is nothing in the detectable signal to detect; the thing you want is categorically not of the kind that detection reaches.

Is there *any* legitimate bridge across the gap? The strongest candidate on the approved list is Searle's, and its limits are instructive. Searle argues that one *can* derive an *ought* from an *is* by way of **institutional facts** — facts that hold only against a background of constitutive rules of the form "X counts as Y in context C" (*How to Derive "Ought" from "Is,"* 1964; *The Construction of Social Reality*, 1995) [high]. Promising is the model: uttering certain words *counts as* undertaking an obligation, and so from the *is* of the utterance one reaches the *ought* of the obligation. This is a real bridge — the strongest there is — but it carries only a restricted load, in two ways that matter for engineering authorization.

First, the *ought* it yields is **conventional and defeasible**, not categorical. It holds in virtue of an institution that could have been otherwise and can be overridden; it is an *ought* relative to the rules of a practice, not an unconditioned one. An authorization grounded this way is only as binding as the institution that constitutes it, and inherits all of that institution's defeasibility.

Second — and this is the load-bearing restriction — Searle's bridge works **promiser-side, not receiver-side.** It binds because the obligated party *undertook* the obligation: the *ought* is self-imposed, generated by the agent's own constitutive act. It does *not* license generating an obligation on someone *else* by performing an institutional act *upon* them. The promiser binds himself; he does not, by the same machinery, bind the one promised. Authorization is exactly the receiver-side case in disguise: to treat an authenticated signal as authorizing an action *upon* a referent is to try to generate a binding *ought* on the *imposed* side, where Searle's bridge does not reach. The institutional move legitimates *self-undertaken* obligation; it does not legitimate *imposed* obligation. So even the strongest bridge leaves the engineering problem standing: authorization is will *self-given*, and you cannot manufacture it from outside by a constitutive stipulation.

Austin sharpens the same edge from the side of performatives. A performative utterance does something rather than reporting something, but only under **felicity conditions**; absent them, the utterance does not succeed but *misfires* or is *etiolated* (*How to Do Things with Words*, 1962) [high]. The wedding words said by an actor on stage, or by one already married, or with no authority to officiate, effect nothing — they are *hollow*, parasitic, void. The lesson, transposed, is decisive: **making-as-such confers nothing.** The mere production of the form — the utterance, the signal, the gesture that *looks like* an authorization — is normatively inert by itself. Only *felicitous, authorized* making confers anything: the performative must be issued by the right party, in the right circumstances, with the right uptake, backing it. An unauthorized "I authorize" is exactly Austin's actor's wedding: a *degenerate constructor*, going through the motions of conferral while conferring nothing. The form is necessary and radically insufficient; what the form lacks is precisely the will-toward-the-referent that no form can contain.

## 4. The Two Design Laws

The metaphysics and the bridge together dictate the form a sound design must take. Two laws follow, and they set up Part III.

**First law: intrinsic-over-extrinsic.** Wherever a design relies on an *extrinsic* check — a separate verification step laid alongside the thing it guards, which can be bypassed, forged, replayed, or simply not consulted — replace it, where possible, with an *intrinsic* property: a property of the construction itself *whose very existence entails the thing the check was trying to establish.* The contrast is the contrast of the chapter. An extrinsic check is *tending laid beside the seed* and detachable from it; an intrinsic property is *the for built into the form* so that the form cannot exist without it. This is **intrinsic-over-extrinsic** as a design imperative, and it is continuous with the metaphysics: a primitive (the dead, committed abstraction of §1) carries its for *constitutively* — you cannot have the primitive without its for — and that is exactly the security property one wants. Make the guarantee a fact about *what the thing is*, not a fact about *what was checked about it*. Detection is extrinsic and defeasible; constitution is intrinsic and not detachable, because there is nothing to detach.

**Second law: construction-over-detection.** Do not detect the attack; make the attack *unrepresentable*. Detection answers an attack that the system permits one to *express* and then tries to catch; construction arranges that the malformed action *cannot be formed at all* — that there is no state, no input, no path that represents the forbidden thing. This is the constructive correlate of **detection → impossibility**: the strongest defense is not a better detector but an architecture in which the thing to be detected has no representation. Where §3 showed that authorization *cannot be detected* — there being nothing in the signal to detect — construction-over-detection is the constructive answer: if you cannot detect the property you need, build a system in which its *absence* cannot be expressed.

But the second law carries its own discipline, and stating it honestly is the hinge into Part III. **Every impossibility is modulo a construction assumption.** "Unrepresentable" is never unrepresentable *simpliciter*; it is unrepresentable *given* that the construction holds — given the type system is sound, the enclave's isolation intact, the hash collision-resistant, the channel free of the side leak. The impossibility is real but *conditional*: it is an impossibility *relative to* the assumptions the construction rests on, and when those assumptions fail — when the isolation is breached, when the side channel speaks, when the primitive is broken — the unrepresentable becomes representable again. The construction does not abolish the attack; it *relocates* it, from inside the represented space to the seam where the construction meets the world.

This is not a defeat of the second law but its correct statement, and it is where Part III must begin. The work ahead is to specify the constructions — object-capability and the principle of least authority, zero-knowledge, hash trees, fuzzy extractors and cancelable biometrics, the discipline of side channels — and, for each, to name the assumption its impossibility is *modulo*, and to ask what happens at the seam when that assumption gives way. The for cannot be detected; it can only be *constructed for* — and every construction is a wager on an assumption. The remainder of the thesis is the auditing of those wagers.
