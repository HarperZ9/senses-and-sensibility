## B — Qadar, Kasb, and Conferred Sourcehood (the classical source for Part III)

The framework's thesis in Part III — that agency is *conferred sourcehood*, neither libertarian self-origination nor determinist dissolution — is not a modern construction reached after Sapolsky. It is, in its sharpest pre-modern form, the achievement of Islamic kalam working out the relation between *al-qadar* (the divine decree) and human accountability. The Prophet (ﷺ) is foundational here not as a thinker among thinkers but as the source from whom both the article of faith and its lived practice descend. What the schoolmen later systematized as *kasb* is the structural ancestor of conferred sourcehood: the human as the *locus* at which an act is acquired and owned, without being its originating ground.

### 1. Al-qadar as an article of *iman*

Belief in the decree is not a marginal scholastic addition; it is enumerated among the very pillars of faith. In the **Hadith of Gabriel** (*Hadith Jibril*), the angel comes to the Prophet (ﷺ) in the form of a man and asks him to define *iman*. The Prophet (ﷺ) answers that it is "to believe in God, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and to believe in the decree, its good and its ill" (*wa tu'mina bi'l-qadari khayrihi wa sharrihi*).

- **Source:** Sahih Muslim, *Kitab al-Iman*, hadith 1 (the famous opening hadith of the collection); the *iman/islam/ihsan* tripartite also appears in Sahih al-Bukhari, *Kitab al-Iman*, though the explicit clause "the decree, its good and its ill" is most prominent in the Muslim recension and in the parallel narration via 'Umar ibn al-Khattab. **Confidence: high** that this is the canonical *Hadith Jibril* in Muslim; **moderate** on attributing the precise *qadar* wording to Bukhari's recension specifically, since the *qadar* clause is the signature of the Muslim version. (The Bukhari text foregrounds the six items of *iman* with the wording emphasis falling differently.)

The theological weight is this: to be a *mu'min* is, in part, to *affirm* that nothing — including one's own acts — escapes the divine decree. This is already, structurally, a no-aseity commitment at the level of creed. The agent's deeds are not exceptions carved out of God's *qudra* (power) as a zone of independent origination. Whatever account of human action follows must be built *inside* this affirmation, not against it. That constraint is precisely what makes the kalam solution non-trivial: it must secure responsibility *without* granting the human an autonomous causal source.

### 2. The three positions and the orthodox middle

The problem space sorts into three classical positions:

**(a) Jabriyya — compulsion.** The *jabr* (coercion) party, associated in the heresiographies (e.g., al-Shahrastani's *al-Milal wa'l-Nihal*) with figures such as Jahm ibn Safwan, held that the human has no real agency at all: acts are attributed to a person only metaphorically, as motion is attributed to a stone that falls. God is the sole agent; the human is the site of God's action and nothing more. **Confidence: high** on the doctrine's shape; **moderate** on the precise attributions, which the heresiographical sources sometimes flatten polemically. In framework terms, *jabr* is the **dissolution** horn: the locus is so thoroughly passive that there is no one home to bear responsibility. The decree swallows the self.

**(b) Qadariyya / Mu'tazila — libertarian self-determination.** Counter-intuitively, the *Qadariyya* are named not for affirming *qadar* but (by the contested traditionalist etymology) for what amounts to its denial in the domain of human acts: they held that the human *creates* (*khalq*) his own voluntary actions. On the Mu'tazili doctrine — systematized by figures such as 'Abd al-Jabbar in his *al-Mughni* — God endows the human with a real *qudra* (a *power before the act*, *al-qudra qabla al-fi'l*) by which the human genuinely originates the deed. This was driven by their theodicy: divine justice (*'adl*) requires that God not be the author of the very acts for which He punishes. **Confidence: high** on 'Abd al-Jabbar and the *qudra*-before-the-act doctrine. This is the **libertarian self-origination** horn: the human is a true *muhdith* (originator), a small creator within his sphere. The cost, from the orthodox side, is a breach in *tawhid* — a second creator, and a region of being not spoken by *kun fayakun*.

**(c) Ash'ari *kasb* — acquisition, the middle.** Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 324/935–936), in *al-Luma'* and as reported in the creedal *al-Ibana*, threaded the needle with the doctrine of **kasb** (acquisition): **God creates the act (*khalq*); the human acquires it (*kasb*).** The act, as an event in being, is created by God's power. But it occurs *conjoined with* a created power (*qudra muhdatha*) in the human, and by that conjunction the act is the human's *acquisition* — his, ascribed to him, the ground of his reward and punishment. The human does not *originate* the act; he is the one *at* whom and *through* whom it is acquired and owned. **Confidence: high** on al-Ash'ari and the *khalq*/*kasb* distinction; this is textbook kalam.

The notorious difficulty — pressed already by the Mu'tazila and conceded to be obscure — is to give *kasb* enough content that it is not merely *jabr* with a softer name. Al-Ash'ari's own statements leave the "acquired power" doing thin causal work, which is why critics called it *kasb* a distinction without a difference. The point to hold for the framework is not that kalam fully *solved* the mechanism, but that it correctly *located* the solution: in a tertium quid between origination and dissolution.

**Maturidi nuance.** Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 333/944), the Samarqandi counterpart to al-Ash'ari, sharpened the human side. In the doctrine associated with his school (and developed in works such as *Kitab al-Tawhid*), the **act of creation** belongs to God, but the human possesses a real *ikhtiyar* (choice) and his own *fi'l* (doing) — Maturidis distinguished God's *creating* (*khalq*) from the human's *acquiring/doing*, and located moral responsibility in an *al-juz' al-ikhtiyari*, the human's undetermined increment of choice in *directing* the created power toward one of two open alternatives. The Maturidi formula is often summarized: the one *fi'l* (action) has two relations — as *created* it is God's; as *acquired/chosen* it is the servant's. **Confidence: high** on the Maturidi school's stronger affirmation of *ikhtiyar* relative to the Ash'aris; **moderate** on the exact technical vocabulary (*al-juz' al-ikhtiyari* is more prominent in later, including post-classical and Indian-subcontinent, Maturidi systematization than in al-Maturidi's own *Kitab al-Tawhid*).

**The structural claim — kasb *is* conferred sourcehood.** Lay the doctrine against the framework's two rejected poles. Libertarian self-origination = the human is a *khaliq* of his acts (Mu'tazila); determinist dissolution = the human is a falling stone (Jabriyya). *Kasb* names the locus where neither holds: the act's *being* is conferred — created, spoken, sustained by God — and yet it is *the human's act*, acquired at and owned by him, such that he is justly answerable for it. The human is not the *source of the act's existence* (that would be aseity, a creaturely *kun*); he is the *conferred source of its ascription* — the standpoint at which a divinely-created act becomes *this person's* doing. That is precisely "agency neither libertarian self-origination nor determinism's dissolution." Responsibility is grounded *without* aseity, because acquisition, not origination, is what grounds it.

This dovetails with **al-Ghazali's occasionalism** (Part I's *re-spoken each instant*). If God re-creates the world's accidents at each moment, then the human's act, too, is re-created at its instant; *kasb* is what makes that re-created act *ascribable*. The framework's "occasionalism of agency" is just the Ash'ari synthesis read forward: existence conferred instant-by-instant (occasionalism), ascription conferred at the locus (kasb).

### 3. The lived synthesis: "tie your camel, then trust in God"

Doctrine becomes practice in a single Prophetic teaching. A man asked the Prophet (ﷺ) whether he should leave his camel untied and trust in God (*tawakkul*); the Prophet (ﷺ) replied: **"Tie her and trust in God"** — *i'qilha wa tawakkal*.

- **Source:** Jami' al-Tirmidhi, *Kitab Sifat al-Qiyama*, hadith **2517**, on the authority of Anas ibn Malik. **Confidence: high** that this hadith is in al-Tirmidhi via Anas; **moderate** on the exact hadith number (numbering varies by edition — Tuhfat al-Ahwadhi vs. printed editions differ slightly). Al-Tirmidhi grades the chain *gharib*; later scholars (including al-Albani) judged it *hasan*. I do *not* claim it is in the two Sahihs.

The teaching is the operational form of *kasb*. *Tawakkul* (reliance/trust) is **not** the Jabriyya posture of leaving the camel untied — that mistakes the conferred standpoint for passivity, as if affirming the decree meant abolishing one's own acquired act. Nor is it the Mu'tazili posture of tying the camel *as if one's own causal power were the real and sufficient ground* of its safety. It is: **act fully — tie the camel with every means at your disposal — and refer the outcome's *being* to the One who confers it.** One labors *within* the decree, not outside it and not instead of it. Effort and trust are not rivals competing for a share of a single causal pie; they sit at different levels — the human's acquired doing, and God's creating — exactly as *kasb* says. *Tawakkul*, properly understood, is the *inhabiting* of the conferred standpoint: doing all that acquisition requires, while never mistaking acquisition for origination.

### 4. Connection to the corpus result: determinism is orthogonal to the conferred standpoint

Here the section closes the loop with Part III's load-bearing claim: **determinism (qadar) is orthogonal to the conferred standpoint.**

The kalam tradition demonstrates this orthogonality experimentally, in creed. It affirms *al-qadar* in its strongest form — *khayrihi wa sharrihi*, good and ill, nothing outside the decree, God the sole *Khaliq* even of human acts — and *in the same breath* affirms full, just human responsibility through *kasb* and *ikhtiyar*. If maximal decree and real accountability were on the *same axis*, this would be a flat contradiction, and the Mu'tazila were right to feel the pressure. The orthodox achievement is to show they are on *different axes*. The question "is the act decreed/created by God?" (answer: yes, entirely) and the question "is the act *yours*, acquired and owned, such that you answer for it?" (answer: yes, entirely) do not trade off. Decree governs the act's *being*; the conferred standpoint governs its *ascription*. That is orthogonality, stated four centuries before the modern free-will debate framed the same point as a category error.

This is what lets the framework **bracket the modern hard determinist on the far side.** Sapolsky's argument (*Determined*, 2023) runs: every act is fully caused by prior states; therefore no one is the *originating source* of their acts; therefore desert/responsibility dissolves. The kalam tradition grants the **first** premise *more* totally than Sapolsky does — not merely physical causal closure but *creation* of the act by God — and yet denies the conclusion, because it never located responsibility in *origination* to begin with. It located it in *acquisition at the conferred locus*. Sapolsky and the Jabriyya stand, structurally, on the *same* horn: both infer dissolution from non-origination. The Mu'tazila and the modern libertarian stand on the *other* horn: both rescue responsibility only by smuggling in origination. **Conferred sourcehood is what both horns point toward and miss** — the third standpoint where you are answerable not because you are the uncaused source, but because the act is conferred *as yours*.

And so the framework's name for the lived posture is already the tradition's name. **Islam — *islam*, submission — is the inhabiting of the conferred standpoint under decree.** Not submission as the stone's passivity (Jabriyya), and not submission as a bargain between two creators (Mu'tazila), but submission as the free acquisition of one's acts *as* gifts spoken into being — tying the camel as fully as if everything depended on the act, and entrusting the outcome as fully as if nothing did, because in truth its *being* is conferred and its *doing* is yours. The Prophet (ﷺ) gives both the article (*qadar* among the pillars of *iman*) and its enactment (*i'qilha wa tawakkal*); the schoolmen give the grammar (*kasb*, *ikhtiyar*); and Part III gives the formal name. The theology is the illumination of the structure, not its premise — but the structure was first walked, then believed, then reasoned, in exactly that order, in this tradition.
