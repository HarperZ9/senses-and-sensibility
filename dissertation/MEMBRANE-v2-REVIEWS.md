# Membrane Paper v2 - Referee Reviews

## Lens: metaethicist - verdict: needs-work

**Strongest objection:** Section 4.3 correctly locates the live normative entry at the human act of policy stipulation and characterizes that act as prescriptive in the full Hare/Hudson endorsement sense — "subscribing." But §7.2 then disclaims: "the framing does not claim that the authorization 'ought' has prescriptive force on human agents." These two passages directly contradict each other. If the stipulation act is prescriptive (as the boomerang resolution requires it to be), then the framing does make a prescriptive claim — about the human stipulator, not about the PDP verdict. The disclaimer in §7.2 is over-broad: it denies prescriptive force at the wrong level of the analysis. A hostile referee will read §4.3 and §7.2 in sequence and conclude the paper is trying to have it both ways: it needs the prescriptive stipulation-act to defeat Searle via Hare/Hudson, and it needs the no-prescriptive-force disclaimer to stay within the safe "weak-ought" scope. That combination is incoherent. The fix is narrow, not structural: §7.2 must be rewritten to disclaim prescriptive force specifically for the PDP's permission verdict, not for the authorization domain in general. As written, the disclaimer partially undercuts the boomerang resolution the paper correctly executed.

### Remaining Issues

**Issue 1 (substantive, requires a sentence-level fix): §7.2 contradicts §4.3 on prescriptive force.**

Section 4.3 correctly locates the live normative entry at the human act of policy stipulation, characterizing it as prescriptive in the Hare/Hudson endorsement sense ("subscribing"). This is necessary to make the boomerang resolution work: the act of stipulating a policy is a genuine normative commitment, not merely a brute institutional fact. Section 7.2 then states: "the framing does not claim that the authorization 'ought' has prescriptive force on human agents."

These two claims are in direct tension. The stipulation-act is a human act, and §4.3 says it is prescriptive. So the framing does make a prescriptive claim — about the human stipulator. §7.2's disclaimer is over-broad: it denies prescriptive force at the wrong level. The intended disclaimer is narrower: the PDP's permission verdict has no prescriptive force (correct and important). The broader disclaimer — that no authorization "ought" has prescriptive force on humans — is false given §4.3's own argument.

Fix: revise §7.2 to read something like "the framing does not claim that the policy's permission verdict — the PDP's 'permit' output — has prescriptive force on human agents. The verdict is a policy-relative classification; it binds no one and motivates nothing directly. The live prescriptive entry appears one level up: at the human act of policy stipulation, which is where the Hare/Hudson endorsement analysis applies (§4.3)." This makes the disclaimer precise rather than over-broad and removes the contradiction.

**Issue 2 (presentational, requires a heading change): §6.2 heading contradicts its own body.**

The section header "The failure taxonomy as category errors" commits to the "category error" characterization, but the body of §6.2 immediately qualifies that "under the weak, policy-relative reading (which this paper endorses for the authz domain), the failures are better described as invalid inferences within a deontic system." If the weak reading is the paper's endorsed position, then "category errors" is not the paper's own term — it is the strong-reading gloss being reported and then rejected. A paper should not organize a section under a heading it immediately walks back in the first paragraph.

Fix: retitle §6.2 to something like "The failure taxonomy: deontic inference failures" or "Structural failures at the is/ought seam," and keep "category error" as the strong-ought characterization the paper acknowledges without endorsing. This resolves the contradiction between heading and body.

**Issue 3 (minor, one sentence): §4.2 ambiguous phrasing creates a circular-sounding inference.**

The sentence "someone decided this ought to be so and institutionalized that decision as the policy" is ambiguous. "Decided this ought to be so" could be read as: (a) the human made a prescriptive normative commitment (what §4.3 intends, and what the Hare/Hudson analysis requires) or (b) a normative conclusion somehow followed from their decision (circular). The phrasing should be sharpened to make the prescriptive-commitment reading unambiguous: "someone made a normative commitment about what should be permitted and institutionalized that commitment as policy" removes the ambiguity.

### What is sound

The paper's core moves are all sound:

- The Pigden reframing correctly identifies which result is in play: conservativeness of logic, not the full Humean categorical gap. This is the right size for the claim.
- The ABLP distancing is precise and defensible. The gap between "ABLP shows that a bridge premise is required" and "this paper names the logical type of that premise as deontic" is real and worth stating.
- The boomerang resolution — concede the weak ought, locate the live normative entry at human stipulation — is the correct philosophical move. It does not dodge the difficulty; it resolves it honestly.
- The Pigdenian point that conservativeness-of-logic applies regardless of deontic modality (the footnote in §4.3) is correct and important: the autonomy of the deontic holds for weak permissions just as it holds for categorical obligations. This prevents the "you only have a trivial premise-need" objection from landing.
- The ocap relocation argument is sound.
- The unmeasurability result follows directly from the Pigdenian framing: if the gap is logical, no measurement improvement closes it. This is the strongest engineering payload the paper generates.
- Confidence labeling is appropriately deployed throughout.

### What Works

**The ABLP distancing (§3.2) is the paper's best move.** It is precise, falsifiable, and exactly right: ABLP shows *that* a bridge premise is required; this paper names the *type* of that premise. That sentence is load-bearing and the paper earns it.

**The Searle/Hare/Hudson adjudication (§4) is now structurally correct.** The boomerang is named, not concealed. The resolution — concede the weak/inert ought, claim Pigden not Hume, locate the live normative entry at human policy stipulation — is the right architecture and it holds. §4.4 ("Searle relocates, does not eliminate") is a clean one-line summary that a venue editor can follow.

**The ocap relocation (§5)** is convincing. The "materialized grant" framing — an ought made bearer-portable — and the ACL/capability parity argument (same seam, different recording location) are both sound and not mere assertion. §5.3 explicitly handles the asymmetry between the two architectures and explains it in terms the deontic framing predicts.

**The unmeasurability result (§6.1)** does real argumentative work. The claim that the gap is logical rather than epistemic — that no signal-property improvement introduces the deontic operator — is genuinely a consequence of the deontic naming that the engineering slogan does not generate. The engineering slogan treats authn and authz as sequential pipeline stages; the unmeasurability result shows they are not the same kind of operation. This survives a hostile referee.

**Confidence labeling is honest.** The negative-search claim is labeled moderate, scoped to literatures surveyed, and flagged as potentially defeatable by a manual pass. The Pigden citation carries an explicit page-number caveat. Jones and Sergot carries a moderate confidence label on venue. This is the right posture.

### Problem 1 (Structural — Major): The failure taxonomy does not demonstrate that it is a *consequence* of the deontic naming

The paper claims in §3.6 and §9 that the failure taxonomy follows "only from" the deontic naming, not from the engineering description alone. This is the paper's second payload and it is where the argument is thinnest.

The three cases in §6.2 are correctly described, and the deontic vocabulary maps onto them cleanly. But the paper never argues — only asserts — that the mapping adds diagnostic content. Consider:

- Hardy (1988) fully characterizes the confused-deputy pattern without any deontic vocabulary. The engineering description ("a component acting on behalf of a caller using authority not granted by that caller") is already a precise diagnosis.
- The ambient authority pattern is likewise fully characterized in capability-security literature (Miller, Shapiro) without deontic framing.
- The token-as-fact-vs-grant distinction already appears in OAuth 2.0 and XACML architectural discussions as a design-level concern.

The paper's §6.2 scope note partially acknowledges this: under the weak ought, the failures are "invalid inferences within a deontic system" rather than full Rylean category errors. But it does not follow through on what work the deontic framing does that the engineering framing does not.

**What is missing:** a specific argument that the deontic classification (a) unifies the three patterns under a single formal description that the engineering literature treats as three separate failure modes, and/or (b) predicts failure modes that the engineering framing cannot detect until after the fact, and/or (c) generates a design criterion — e.g., "no IS-fact may serve as a direct premise for an authorization conclusion without an explicit deontic bridge" — that can be stated as a checkable property.

If (a) is the claim, the paper needs a sentence showing that the engineering literature actually treats these as distinct rather than as a family. If (b) or (c), the paper needs at least a sketch of the criterion and an example of where it predicts something the engineering diagnosis misses.

Without this, the taxonomy section reads as: "here are three well-known failures, and we can redescribe them in deontic vocabulary." That is not a consequence; it is a translation exercise.

**Fix:** Add one paragraph in §6.2 — before or after the three cases — that makes the unification argument explicit. The simplest version: "These three patterns share a single formal structure — a predicate over IS-facts serving as premise for a permission conclusion — that the engineering descriptions name independently. The deontic framing is the common form; the engineering names are its instances. This is why the category error characterization matters: it explains why all three are vulnerable to the same class of attack (any IS-fact that is mistakenly treated as authoritative for an OUGHT-verdict can be manipulated by controlling the IS-fact)." Then cite at least one case where that common-form diagnosis generates a unified defensive criterion: explicit separation of fact-supply (PIP) from permission-evaluation (PDP), applicable to all three.

### Problem 2 (Structural — Moderate): §2.2 sets up a derivation-from-policy move that is never discharged against the falsification condition

Section 2.2 states: "a policy-relative permission ('permitted under policy P') is in principle derivable from is-facts plus the descriptive fact that policy P is in force." This is the Searle concession, stated clearly. Section 4 then argues that the policy being in force is itself the product of a normative act of stipulation.

But §7.3 (falsification condition) states the framing is refuted "if and only if a coherent access-control architecture can be exhibited in which authorization verdicts are fully and correctly derived from authentication signal-properties alone, with no standing policy conferral." Given the §2.2 concession, the falsification bar is set at the wrong level: a Searle-style system that includes the policy as a descriptive fact does derive authorization verdicts from signal-properties-plus-descriptive-rule, without a live normative premise at evaluation time. The paper's response is §4 — the enactment was normative — but §7.3 does not incorporate that response; it states the falsification condition as if the §2.2 Searle concession had not been made.

This is a consistency gap, not a fatal flaw, but a hostile referee will catch it. The falsification condition as written excludes only architectures with no policy at all. It does not exclude Searle-style systems with a policy-as-descriptive-fact, which is the actual objection the paper needs to foreclose.

**Fix:** Revise §7.3 to incorporate the §4 resolution: the falsification condition is met only by an architecture in which (a) authorization verdicts are derived from signal-properties alone without any standing policy, or (b) the standing policy is itself derivable from signal-properties without any prior normative act of stipulation. Condition (b) is the Searle-style system; the paper argues it fails because the stipulation was itself normative. Making this explicit in §7.3 closes the gap.

### Concrete Fixes (Priority Order)

1. **§6.2 — add the unification argument.** Before the three case analyses, add a paragraph stating: (a) that the engineering literature treats these three as distinct failure patterns, (b) that the deontic framing reveals their common formal structure — an IS-predicate serving as premise for a permission conclusion — and (c) that this common form generates a unified defensive criterion (PIP/PDP separation as a design invariant, applicable across all three). Without this, the taxonomy is restatement, not consequence.

2. **§7.3 — revise the falsification condition** to explicitly foreclose the Searle-style architecture (policy as descriptive fact, derivation at evaluation time). The current formulation is weaker than the paper's actual argument requires.

3. **§6.2 scope note — tighten the language.** The current note says under the weak ought the failures are "invalid inferences within a deontic system." If Problem 1 is fixed and the unification argument is made, this note should be revised to say: under the weak ought, the failures are instances of a single formal error (IS-predicate as authorization premise) that the deontic framing identifies as a structural class, enabling a class-level defensive criterion rather than failure-by-failure engineering patches.

4. **§3.2 — one sentence addition.** After "That is the gap this paper claims to fill," add: "ABLP's non-entailment result is a necessary condition for the Membrane Framing — it establishes that the gap exists; the Membrane Framing names what fills it (a deontic permission) and derives consequences from that naming." This makes the logical relationship between ABLP and the paper's contribution explicit for referees who know the ABLP literature well.

5. **References — Miller et al. (2003).** The moderate-confidence citation for the capability-myths technical report is the weakest entry in the references. If a peer-reviewed venue is targeted, consider whether this citation is load-bearing or can be replaced with the more widely cited Dennis and Van Horn (1966) plus a reference to the capability security literature survey.
