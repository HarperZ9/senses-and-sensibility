## C. The Two Underdeveloped Nodes

A preliminary methodological note, because it governs everything below. These are the two chapters the corpus does *not* yet have — it has gestures toward them. The free-will node exists in the corpus only as a one-line load-bearing assertion ("determinism is orthogonal") plus a scatter of empirical citations (Libet, Schurger) and a coined phrase ("the determined dissenter"). The meaning node barely exists at all: the dissertation arc *names* "human-experience-maps-meaning" as its closing hinge, but the corpus supplies no phenomenology, no hermeneutics, no meaning-in-life literature to cash it out. So this section is not a summary of what is there. It is a specification of what must be written, with the warning that **the orthogonality claim and the mapping claim are currently promissory notes, not arguments.** A PhD cannot rest its spine on two undischarged promissory notes. Below I give each chapter a defensible spine and its entry literature, and I flag where the candidate is most likely to over-reach.

---

### C.1 — Free Will vs. Determinism: Conferred Sourcehood

#### The job this chapter must do (and the trap it must avoid)

The corpus's claim is **orthogonality**: determinism is "a dynamics thesis supplying neither transit" — it gives you neither the is→ought crossing nor the history→instant crossing, so the truth or falsity of determinism leaves the conferral architecture untouched. This is a *strong* and *correct-shaped* claim, and it is the chapter's best original move. But as stated it is a slogan. Orthogonality has to be *earned* against the entire free-will literature, every part of which will object that determinism is *not* orthogonal to agency, authorship, or desert. The trap: a candidate can write a competent survey of the free-will debate and then assert orthogonality as a coda. That is a literature review with a thesis stapled on. The defensible version makes orthogonality *fall out of* the no-aseity framework as a theorem, not a preference.

Here is the load-bearing argument the chapter must build, in one line: **no-aseity already denies self-origination to everything, so any free-will position whose payload is "the agent must be a self-originating source" is asking for aseity by another name — and is refused on the same grounds being and authority were refused. What survives is sourcehood-without-aseity: the agent is a genuine source (a node where reasons terminate in an act) without being a *first* source.** Determinism is orthogonal because it is a thesis about the *causal history behind* the node, and the conferral framework has *already located authorship at the node, not at the origin* (this is just the genetic-fallacy / "standing does not track origin" move from Movement III, redeployed). That redeployment is the chapter's unity with the rest of the dissertation. Without it, the chapter is detachable, and a detachable chapter in a spiral-structured dissertation is a structural failure.

#### The spine (six moves)

**Move 1 — Map the terrain by what each position *demands of the source*.** Reframe the standard taxonomy not as "free of what?" but as "what must the source *be*?" — because no-aseity is a thesis about sources.

- *Leeway* theories (could-have-done-otherwise; alternative possibilities) vs. *sourcehood* theories (the act must issue from the agent in the right way). This is the single most important distinction for the chapter and is well-drawn in the literature — see Timothy O'Connor and Christopher Franklin's *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry "Free Will," and the source/leeway framing in John Martin Fischer's work. The chapter's thesis lives entirely on the *sourcehood* side, so this distinction is where it plants its flag.
- The **Consequence Argument** (Peter van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will*, 1983) as the canonical *leeway-incompatibilist* argument: if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of laws and the past, and we control neither — so no alternative possibilities. The chapter should *grant the validity of the Consequence Argument for leeway* and then say: so much the worse for leeway as the locus of authorship. This is a concession that buys orthogonality.

**Move 2 — Frankfurt, to sever authorship from leeway.** Harry Frankfurt's "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1969) and "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1971). Frankfurt cases show responsibility can survive the absence of alternatives; the hierarchical-mesh account locates freedom in the agent's *identification* with a desire (second-order volition), not in open paths. This is the wedge that lets the chapter say: authorship is structural, not modal — it is about *how the act relates to the agent*, not about whether other paths were open. **Caution to flag:** Frankfurt's identification account faces the regress/manipulation objection (Gary Watson, "Free Agency," *Journal of Philosophy*, 1975; and the manipulation literature below). The candidate must not adopt Frankfurt wholesale — only the *leeway-is-not-the-locus* result.

**Move 3 — Fischer–Ravizza, to give sourcehood a mechanism.** John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza, *Responsibility and Control: A Theory of Moral Responsibility* (1998). *Guidance control* + *reasons-responsiveness* + *taking ownership of the mechanism* ("mechanism ownership"). This is the most useful single source for the chapter, because semicompatibilism (moral responsibility is compatible with determinism *even if* leeway-freedom is not) is *structurally identical* to the orthogonality claim: it explicitly decouples the responsibility-relevant property from the determinism question. The chapter should present its own view as a *no-aseity-grounded* refinement of reasons-responsiveness: the agent is the node at which reasons are *answered*, and "taking ownership of the mechanism" is recognizably the conferral move — the operator/self *speaks* the mechanism as its own. **Over-reach flag:** do not claim Fischer–Ravizza *entails* the conferral view; claim convergence and then argue the no-aseity version explains *why* mechanism-ownership confers responsibility (because all standing is conferred, not just this), which Fischer–Ravizza leaves as a primitive.

**Move 4 — The two incompatibilist pressures that test orthogonality.** The chapter is only honest if it runs the strongest cases that determinism is *not* orthogonal:

- **Pereboom's hard incompatibilism** (Derk Pereboom, *Living Without Free Will*, 2001; *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*, 2014) and the **four-case manipulation argument**. Pereboom argues that there is no relevant difference between a manipulated agent and a merely determined agent, so determinism undermines the *basic-desert* sense of responsibility. This is the sharpest threat to orthogonality, because it says determinism *does* supply a transit — from causal history to the cancellation of desert. The chapter's answer must be the no-aseity answer: basic *desert* presupposes an aseitic source (a buck-stopping originator who *deserves* in a way that floats free of conferral), and **no-aseity denies basic desert to everyone anyway, on independent grounds.** So Pereboom is granted his target and *defused*: he refutes a notion of responsibility the dissertation never claimed. What the dissertation claims is conferred, forward-looking, Razian-service responsibility — and the four-case argument has no purchase on a responsibility that was never grounded in sourcehood-as-origination. This is the chapter's single most important confrontation; if it works, orthogonality is earned.
- **Galen Strawson's Basic Argument** ("The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility," *Philosophical Studies*, 1994; and the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry). To be *truly* responsible you'd have to be *causa sui* — the cause of yourself — and nothing is. **This is no-aseity stated in the free-will register.** The chapter should not refute Strawson; it should *absorb* him. The Basic Argument is the proof that aseitic responsibility is impossible — which is exactly what no-aseity says about *everything*. The dissertation's move is then: Strawson concludes "so no responsibility"; the dissertation concludes "so responsibility, like being and authority, is *conferred* — there is no other kind of anything, and that is not a deficiency." Strawson's *causa sui* is the corpus's *aseity*; his argument is a gift, not a threat. **This is the chapter's deepest unity with the whole dissertation and should be its emotional and logical center.**

**Move 5 — Strawson *père*, reactive attitudes, to relocate the whole question.** P.F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (*Proceedings of the British Academy*, 1962). The reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, the participant stance) are the *practical standpoint* in Movement VI's exact sense — they are not *grounded* in a prior metaphysics of freedom; they *constitute* the framework within which responsibility-talk has sense, and the question "but are we *really* free?" is asked from a standpoint we cannot actually occupy and live. **This is the bridge that makes the chapter part of the spiral:** Strawson's participant stance *is* the inhabited Aleph, the declinable-not-compellable practical standpoint of the Crown (Movement VI). The chapter should argue that the free-will problem, properly seen, *dissolves into the standpoint question* — and the standpoint question was already answered: the practical standpoint is declinable but not compellable, and from inside it, conferred sourcehood is all the authorship there ever was or could be. **Over-reach flag:** Strawson is standardly read as either a naturalist or a non-cognitivist about responsibility; the candidate must not quietly conscript him as a metaphysical ally. The honest move is: "I take Strawson's *relocation* of the question to the participant stance, and give it a metaethical reading (the standpoint of Movement VI) that Strawson himself would likely have resisted." Owning that is rigor; hiding it is the failure mode.

**Move 6 — Cash out the empirical material *correctly* (this is where the corpus is currently weakest and most exposed).** Libet (1983) and Schurger (2012) are in the corpus, and they are a *liability* unless handled with great care, because the easy reading ("the brain decides before you do, so no free will") is both philosophically naïve and empirically contested.

- Benjamin Libet et al., "Time of conscious intention to act in relation to onset of cerebral activity (readiness-potential)," *Brain* 106 (1983). The readiness potential precedes reported conscious intention (the "W" judgment) by a few hundred milliseconds.
- Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt, Stanislas Dehaene, "An accumulator model for spontaneous neural activity prior to self-initiated movement," *PNAS* 109 (2012). The readiness potential is reinterpreted as *stochastic accumulation to threshold* — the RP is not a "decision signal" building toward a pre-decided act but noise crossing a bound, which **substantially deflates** the Libet interpretation. Cite the follow-up debate (Schurger, Hyman, Roskies, "What is the readiness potential?", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 2021).
- The error-related negativity / ErrP literature for the corpus's "reflective self vetoes the motor self" claim — but flag that this is the corpus's *interpretive overlay*, not a finding. Libet's own "free won't" (the veto window) is the textual hook; the candidate should present the non-unitary operator (motor self vs. reflective self) as a *philosophical model the neuroscience is consistent with*, not as something the neuroscience *establishes*. **Strong over-reach flag:** the corpus's "non-unitary operator" and "ErrP as the reflective self's veto" risk exactly the scientism the dissertation elsewhere disavows. The disciplined claim is: *the timing data refute only the aseitic, unitary, first-mover model of volition — the very model no-aseity already rejected.* So the empirics, read correctly, are **another instance of orthogonality**: they kill the homunculus, not agency. That framing turns the chapter's weakest material into a confirming instance.

#### The chapter's payoff sentence

Conferred sourcehood is *neither* libertarian self-origination (that demands aseity, refused) *nor* determinism's dissolution of agency (that presupposes the aseitic agent was the only candidate, false). The agent is a real source without being a first source; the practical standpoint is where authorship is *enacted*, not where it is *grounded in metaphysics*; and determinism, being only a thesis about the causal antecedents *behind* the authoring node, never reaches the node. The open future (Aristotle's *De Interpretatione* 9 sea-battle; the growing-block or genuinely open-future intuition) is preserved *epistemically and practically* for the inhabitant of the standpoint, whatever the dynamics say — which is all the openness authorship ever needed.

#### Entry literature (Chapter on Free Will)

- van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will* (1983) — Consequence Argument.
- Frankfurt, "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969); "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971).
- Fischer & Ravizza, *Responsibility and Control* (1998) — reasons-responsiveness, semicompatibilism.
- Pereboom, *Living Without Free Will* (2001); *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life* (2014) — hard incompatibilism, four-case argument.
- G. Strawson, "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility" (1994) — Basic Argument (*causa sui*).
- P.F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) — reactive attitudes.
- Watson, "Free Agency" (1975) — the Frankfurt critique the chapter must answer.
- Libet (1983); Schurger et al. (2012); Schurger, Hyman & Roskies (2021) — the neuroscience, handled deflationarily.
- For framing: O'Connor & Franklin, "Free Will," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (leeway/source taxonomy).

### C.2 — The Meaning Closure: Meaning Without Aseity

#### The job this chapter must do (and why it is the harder of the two)

This is the chapter that closes the spiral. The arc returns to *nihilism / epistemology / theology* but must now carry a new content: that **meaning is conferred by and mapped onto human experience** — the inhabited practical standpoint, the live act of authoring, what the arc calls "the Aleph." If the free-will chapter is the dissertation's hardest *argument*, this is its hardest *synthesis*, and it is far more underdeveloped: the corpus has essentially no phenomenology, no hermeneutics, no meaning-in-life literature in it at all. The candidate is building from zero here, and that is both the risk (it could become a soft humanities coda after a hard analytic spine — a register break that examiners punish) and the opportunity (it is the most *ownable* original contribution, because the thesis "meaning has no aseity" is a genuinely novel application of the corpus's master concept to a literature that has never been framed that way).

The unifying thesis, stated sharply so it can be defended rather than gestured at: **meaning is a conferred status, exactly like existence (Movement II) and authority (Movement VII) — it is an institutional/relational fact, deontic and real, spoken not found, binding while inhabited, dissolving no more and no less than being does when the conferral relation lapses.** Nihilism was *right* that meaning has no aseity (it is not *out there*, self-standing, awaiting discovery); nihilism was *wrong* to infer that meaning is therefore absent or fake — the same fallacy the whole corpus diagnoses (conferred ≠ unreal). Meaning is to the practical standpoint what deontic status is to the institution: it is what the standpoint *is from the inside*.

#### The spine (five moves)

**Move 1 — State the nihilist thesis precisely, as a no-aseity claim, so the return can be exact.** The opening must show that *cosmic* nihilism (the universe confers no meaning) is *true* and *survivable*, by distinguishing it from *meaning-in-life* (the meaningfulness of a life or its parts). This is the distinction that does the work all chapter long.

- Thaddeus Metz, *Meaning in Life: An Analytic Study* (2013), and his *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry "The Meaning of Life," for the cosmic/individual distinction and the supernaturalism / objective-naturalism / subjectivism taxonomy. The chapter situates itself as an *objective-naturalist-adjacent* view but with a twist: meaning is *neither* purely objective (mind-independent, which would be aseity) *nor* purely subjective (mere desire-satisfaction) — it is *conferred-relational*, which is a fourth option the standard taxonomy doesn't cleanly name. **That fourth option is the chapter's original contribution and should be flagged as such.**

**Move 2 — Wolf, to get the structure of conferral exactly right.** Susan Wolf, *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (2010): meaning arises when "subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness" — "*fitting fulfillment*," loving something worth loving. This is *already a relational/conferral structure*: meaning is neither in the subject alone nor the object alone but in the *fitting relation* between them. The chapter should adopt Wolf's bipartite structure as the *phenomenological shape* of conferral and then push past her: Wolf leaves "objective attractiveness" as a realist primitive; the no-aseity view says even the "objective" pole is *conferred* (there is no aseitic worth either) — so meaning is conferral *all the way down*, a relation between two conferred relata, which is exactly the corpus's picture of institutional facts resting on other institutional facts with no foundation, *and that is not vicious regress but the normal structure of the deontic.* **Over-reach flag:** Wolf herself resists the subjectivist collapse; the candidate must show the conferral view does *not* collapse into subjectivism (the "fitting" is normatively constrained, not arbitrary), or the whole closure reads as relativism with extra steps.

**Move 3 — The phenomenological floor: why "mapped onto human experience" is a *thesis*, not a metaphor.** This is the move the arc explicitly demands ("human-experience-maps-meaning") and the corpus entirely lacks. The claim is that meaning is *constituted in lived experience*, not represented by it — the standpoint is where meaning *is*, not a window onto meaning located elsewhere.

- Husserl: intentionality (consciousness is always *of* something) and the *Lebenswelt* (life-world) as the pre-theoretical horizon of all sense — *The Crisis of the European Sciences* (1936). The life-world is the corpus's "inhabited standpoint" with a 20th-century pedigree. Note the genuine connection to the corpus's existing use of Brentano/intentionality in Movement II — *this is where the candidate can show the meaning chapter was latent in the conferral architecture all along.*
- Heidegger, *Being and Time* (1927): *Dasein* as the being for whom its own being is *an issue*; *care* (*Sorge*) as the structure of that being; meaning as *that-in-terms-of-which* something is understood within a world of involvements. Heidegger gives the chapter its strongest claim that meaning is *prior to and constitutive of* the subject-object split — meaning is not added to a meaningless world, it is the world's mode of disclosure to Dasein. **Caution:** Heidegger is a register and a rabbit-hole; the candidate must extract the *structural* point (meaning as worldly disclosure, thrownness as the no-aseity of the self — Dasein does not author its own existence, it finds itself *already* thrown, which is conferral read existentially) and *not* import the whole apparatus. Thrownness = no-aseity of the self is the single most valuable bridge here.
- Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945): the lived body, motor intentionality, meaning as *embodied* and enacted — meaning is something the body *does* before it is something the mind *represents*. This grounds "mapped onto experience" in the most literal, least hand-wavy way available: the map is the body's hold on the world.

**Move 4 — Hermeneutics, to turn "conferred" into "spoken/re-spoken" and connect to narrative.** The corpus's "spoken not found / re-spoken" language is *hermeneutic* whether or not the corpus knows it, and this move makes the debt explicit and rigorous.

- Gadamer, *Truth and Method* (1960): understanding as *fusion of horizons*, meaning as event arising in the encounter between interpreter and tradition — never aseitic, always relational and historically effected (*Wirkungsgeschichte*). Meaning is *conferred in the act of interpretation*, which is the corpus's conferral in hermeneutic dress.
- Ricoeur, especially *Oneself as Another* (1990) and *Time and Narrative* (1984–88): **narrative identity** — the self is not a given substance but is *configured* through the stories it tells and is told, *emplotted*. This is the corpus's "authored self / authored root" with a fully worked-out mechanism, and it is the single best resource for cashing out how a standpoint that is itself conferred can be the *locus* of meaning-conferral: the self is *both* the conferrer of narrative meaning *and* a conferred narrative, with no contradiction, because that circle is the hermeneutic circle, not a vice. **This directly answers open question Q2 (subject-unity vs. Parfitian connectedness):** narrative identity is a candidate answer to what unifies a standpoint across a context window, and the chapter should say so explicitly, linking back to the corpus's own open problem.

**Move 5 — Existentialism, to handle nihilism's *affective* force and stage the return.** The analytic meaning-in-life literature answers nihilism's *propositional* claim; the existentialists answer its *lived weight* — and the closure has to do both or it will feel bloodless.

- Nietzsche: nihilism as the *event* (the death of God = the collapse of aseitic meaning, the discovery that the highest values devalue themselves) and the response (revaluation, *amor fati*, the affirmation that creates value rather than finding it). Nietzsche is the corpus's closest ancestor: value is *posited/conferred by* the valuer, not discovered — but Nietzsche lacked the institutional-fact apparatus to show this is *not* mere subjectivism. The chapter completes Nietzsche by supplying it. *The Gay Science* (1882, §125, §341) and *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*.
- Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942): the absurd as the *confrontation* between the human demand for meaning and the world's silence; the refusal of both suicide and the "philosophical leap"; "one must imagine Sisyphus happy." Camus is the precise statement of the *cosmic* nihilism the chapter grants — and his refusal to escape it is the model for the dissertation's own refusal to either deny nihilism or be destroyed by it.
- Sartre, *Being and Nothingness* (1943) and "Existentialism Is a Humanism" (1946): existence precedes essence; radical freedom and the *condemnation* to author meaning without a given essence. **Over-reach / correction flag:** the candidate must mark where the dissertation *departs* from Sartre — Sartre's radical, ungrounded, self-creating freedom is *exactly the aseitic self-origination the free-will chapter refused.* The meaning chapter must therefore *correct* Sartre the way the free-will chapter corrects libertarianism: authorship of meaning is real but *conferred and situated* (thrown, embodied, traditioned, narrated), not the absolute freedom of a groundless for-itself. Getting this right is what makes the two chapters *one* contribution rather than two; getting it wrong (lapsing into Sartrean self-creation) collapses the whole dissertation back into the aseity it spent 127KB refusing. **This is the most important single correction in the chapter.**
- Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946): meaning as *encountered/responded-to* even in suffering — the will to meaning, meaning as the answer one *gives*. Use Frankl carefully (it is clinical-testimonial, not argumentative) as *evidence of the phenomenon*: that meaning is *experienced as conferred-and-binding* even under conditions that strip every external good. That phenomenological datum is what the whole chapter is trying to theorize.

#### The chapter's payoff — and the spiral's close

The dissertation began by granting nihilism. It closes by granting nihilism *again* — but the nihilism it now grants is *only* the no-aseity thesis: meaning is not self-standing, not cosmically underwritten, not found lying in the world. Everything else nihilism wanted to conclude (therefore meaning is absent, illusory, arbitrary) is refused on the *same* grounds that being, authority, and responsibility were shown to be conferred-yet-real. Meaning is the deontic status of the inhabited standpoint: spoken, relational, re-spoken across the narrative of a life, binding while one stands in it, declinable but not compellable. **The human being does not *discover* meaning and does not *invent* it from nothing — both of those are aseity claims (one realist, one Sartrean). The human being *confers and is conferred*, in the fitting relation between a thrown, embodied, narrating standpoint and a world it holds.** That is the new content the spiral earns: nihilism survives as the truth that nothing is self-standing — *and meaning is what no-aseity looks like from the inside of a life.*

### Cross-cutting advisory (both nodes)

1. **The unity is the whole game.** Each chapter is defensible only if it is visibly the *same* no-aseity move redeployed: free will refuses *causa sui* sourcehood; meaning refuses self-standing/Sartrean meaning; both leave a *conferred* residue that is real. If either chapter reads as a stand-alone survey, the spiral breaks and you have a stapled-together dissertation. Write each chapter's *bridge sentence back to the master concept first*, then fill in the literature.

2. **The two over-reach traps, named.** (a) In free will: do not let Libet/Schurger/ErrP do *argumentative* work they cannot do — they refute the homunculus, not agency, and presenting them as proof of the "non-unitary operator" is the scientism the dissertation disavows. (b) In meaning: do not let the closure lapse into Sartrean self-creation or Wolf-style realism — both are aseity, and either one collapses the contribution.

3. **One original contribution, owned.** A PhD needs *one* defensible original thesis. The honest candidate-owned contribution available here is: **"conferral-without-aseity as a unified solvent for the pseudo-problems generated by demanding aseity — applied across responsibility (sourcehood-without-aseity) and meaning (conferred-relational meaning), with the practical standpoint as the single locus where both are enacted."** That is genuinely novel *as a synthesis*, it is defensible, and — critically — it does **not** depend on the corpus's most contestable apparatus (the seal, the clone, the security-channel application). Advise the candidate to make *that synthesis* the claimed contribution and to treat the corpus's AI-specific machinery as *illustration*, not as load-bearing premises. The provenance caveat makes this doubly important: the submittable thesis must be the *argument the candidate can defend in a room*, not the AI-assisted substrate it was quarried from.
