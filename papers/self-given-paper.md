# Self-Given Is Not Self-Grounding: Why Perspectival Aseity Dissolves to Seity

---

## Abstract

Can consciousness be the one self-grounding reality — the lone entity that possesses its own existence rather than receiving it? Five philosophical traditions mount that claim in its strongest published forms: Descartes' cogito, Galen Strawson's robust phenomenal subject, the Indic doctrine of *svaprakāśa* (self-luminosity) as developed through Dan Zahavi's pre-reflective self-awareness, Fichte's self-positing absolute I, and Advaita Vedānta's self-established Ātman. This paper tests each tradition against a single distinction — *self-givenness* (epistemic: a thing known from itself, indubitable, self-luminous, intrinsic) versus *self-grounding* (ontological: a thing whose very existence is ungenerated and non-dependent) — and against a master image: heat is intrinsic to a flame, yet the flame depends on fuel. Intrinsic character does not entail aseity of existence. The paper argues that the first four traditions, examined at full strength, deliver self-givenness but not self-grounding, and are thereby vindicated as instances of *seity* — ownerless occurrence, self-given but dependent. Three canonical critiques converge across traditions to produce this result independently of any single theoretical framework: Lichtenberg's correction of the cogito (*es denkt*, not *ich denke*), Kant's First Paralogism, and Candrakīrti's lamp. The fifth tradition — Advaita Vedānta — represents a genuine and undefeated standoff at the ultimate level, which any no-aseity framework must bracket rather than refute. The paper closes by stating the precise honest bound: no aseity in the conferred or conventional order; whether the ultimate ground is a self-luminous aseity is the open Advaita–Madhyamaka question, bracketed but not settled.

**Keywords:** aseity, seity, self-givenness, self-luminosity, cogito, Advaita Vedānta, perspectival consciousness

---

## 1. Introduction

There is a standing temptation in philosophy of mind and in metaphysics to treat consciousness as the one exception to dependence — the single reality that grounds itself rather than receiving its being from elsewhere. The temptation is not arbitrary. First-person experience presents itself with a peculiar forcefulness: it cannot be doubted from the inside in the way that external objects can; it seems to carry its own warrant; it appears, in some sense, to be its own evidence. From Descartes' foundational *cogito ergo sum* through Fichte's absolute self-positing, from the Vedāntic *sākṣin* to the contemporary phenomenologist's pre-reflective self-awareness, the claim takes recognizable shape: consciousness or the first-person subject is not merely the most immediately known thing; it is the self-grounding thing, the one entity whose being does not hang on anything else.

This paper calls that claim *perspectival aseity*. Aseity, in the classical theological and metaphysical tradition, names the property of existing *a se* — from and through oneself, ungenerated, non-dependent for existence on any other entity. The claim of perspectival aseity is that the first-person perspective, the conscious subject, the knowing center — whatever name the tradition gives it — exhibits precisely this property.

The paper's thesis is that perspectival aseity, examined at its five strongest published points, does not deliver aseity. It delivers *seity*. Seity names what remains when aseity is denied to something that is nonetheless not straightforwardly other-determined in the way a stone is: the thing is self-given, ownerless, occurring — but its existence is not self-grounded. It is received or dependent, even if the dependence relation is unusual or difficult to characterize. The apparent perspectival aseity, illuminated rather than suppressed, turns out to be seity: self-given but not self-grounding, ownerless occurrence.

The argument proceeds through five traditions. Sections 3 through 7 take each in turn, at its strongest, and apply the master distinction and the master image. Section 8 presents the two-level result: at the empirical level, the apparent perspectival aseity dissolves to seity; at the ultimate level, one tradition — Advaita Vedānta — produces a genuine standoff that must be bracketed, not refuted. Section 9 states the honest bound with precision, and Section 10 concludes.

One methodological note before proceeding. This paper does not argue that consciousness is unimportant, derivative, or philosophically reducible. The self-givenness of experience — its intrinsic, first-person character, its immunity to certain forms of doubt, its non-relational intimacy — is real and philosophically significant. The argument is a restricted one: that self-givenness, however real, is not the same property as self-grounding, and that the move from the first to the second, which each of the five traditions makes in some form, is not warranted. The distinction matters because conflating the two produces inflated ontological conclusions from epistemological premises.

## 2. The Master Distinction and the Flame

The distinction that decides everything in what follows can be stated compactly.

*Self-givenness* is an epistemic property. A thing is self-given when it is known or warranted from itself — when it does not require an external certification to count as manifest, when its presence is indubitable in some characteristic way, when its character is intrinsic rather than constituted by relations to other things. Self-luminosity in the Indic tradition captures one form of this: a cognition that illuminates its own occurrence rather than requiring a further cognition to reveal it. Immunity-to-error-through-misidentification, in Sydney Shoemaker's formulation, captures an analytic-philosophy version: first-person reports about one's own mental states cannot be mistaken in the particular way that third-person reports can. The pre-reflective self-awareness that Dan Zahavi draws from Husserl and Sartre captures another: experience is always already given *as mine*, not in a subsequent act of self-attribution but in the very structure of experiencing. All of these are genuine phenomena. The paper does not dispute them.

*Self-grounding*, by contrast, is an ontological property. A thing is self-grounding when it does not depend on anything else for its existence — when it is what the scholastic tradition called *causa sui* in the most literal sense: its existence is not conferred by, derived from, or sustained by any other entity or condition. This is aseity in the strict sense. It is an extremely strong claim. In classical theism it belongs to God alone; in Spinozistic monism it belongs to the single substance. To claim it for the first-person subject or for consciousness is to claim something remarkable.

The confusion of these two properties is the error this paper tracks. The slide from self-givenness to self-grounding is seductive because self-givenness does look like a form of independence — independence from external epistemic warrant, from relational constitution, from the kind of uncertainty that attaches to knowledge of the external world. But epistemic independence of this kind is fully compatible with ontological dependence. A thing can be intrinsically self-presenting while depending for its very existence on conditions outside itself.

The flame image makes this precise. Heat is intrinsic to a flame in a clear sense: it is not a relational property of the flame, not something the flame has in virtue of its relation to something external, but part of what being a flame is. The flame's heat is, one might say, self-given: it does not need to be imported from outside; it is there in the very occurrence of the flame. Yet the flame depends on fuel for its existence. Take away the fuel, and the flame does not merely lose a relational property; it ceases to exist. The intrinsic character of the heat is therefore fully compatible with the dependent existence of the flame. Intrinsic character does not entail aseity of existence.

This image recurs across the five traditions as the diagnostic test. Wherever a tradition offers a consciousness whose character is intrinsic, self-luminous, or self-given, the question is whether that intrinsicality of character establishes non-dependence of existence. In each case except the last, the answer is: it does not.

## 3. The Cogito: Self-Givenness Without a Self-Grounding I

Descartes' *cogito ergo sum* is the prototype of perspectival aseity and the most familiar attempt to derive a self-grounding first-person from the structure of doubt. The argument is well-known: one cannot doubt that one is doubting without performing a doubting; the doubting is a thinking; wherever there is thinking there is an I that thinks; therefore *I am* is incontrovertible, and the I that thinks is established as the one indubitable reality. The apparent aseity lies in the move from indubitability to existence: the I does not merely appear self-given; it seems to establish its own existence by the very act of being doubted away.

The definitive critique of this move was formulated not by Descartes' contemporaries but by Georg Christoph Lichtenberg in the late eighteenth century, in the *Sudelbücher*. Lichtenberg's observation is compact and surgical: the cogito licenses *es denkt* — there is thinking — but not *ich denke* — I think. The indubitable datum is that thinking is occurring now. It does not follow that there is a subject — a substantial, persisting, self-identical I — that owns the thinking. That further inference is exactly what Descartes needs and what the cogito does not supply. The datum of indubitability is impersonal: it registers an occurrence, not an owner of the occurrence. The grammatical subject "I" in *cogito* may be nothing more than the formal requirement that German and Latin verbs take a subject — no more ontologically loaded than the "it" in "it is raining."

Kant arrives at the same point through a different route in the First Paralogism of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. The paralogism, as Kant analyzes it, is the invalid inference from the formal unity of apperception — the "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations — to a substantial, simple, numerically identical soul. The "I think" is a logical condition on the unity of experience, not a datum that reveals a substance. It is, in Kant's careful phrase, a *mere representation* that accompanies; it establishes that representations are mine in the formal sense of belonging to a unified apperception, but it does not establish a self-grounding substantial entity that underlies and explains this unity. The inference to substance is precisely what Kant calls the paralogism: a formally valid-looking argument whose middle term shifts meaning between premises.

Even the strongest reconstructions of the cogito's epistemic force do not overcome this diagnosis. Shoemaker's immunity-to-error-through-misidentification establishes that first-person reports cannot be wrong in a specific way about whose experience is being reported — but this is precisely a self-givenness claim, not a self-grounding claim. It establishes that the experience is given as mine, not that there is a self-grounding mine that generates it. Thomas Nagel's insistence on the subjective character of experience — the fact that there is *something it is like* to be a bat — establishes that experience has an intrinsic, perspectival character irreducible to third-person description, but again this is a claim about the character of the experience, not about the ontological status of the experiencing subject. What is given is the thinking-now, the occurrence-from-here. What is not given, and what the cogito cannot deliver, is a subject that grounds that occurrence in its own being.

The cogito, then, vindicates seity rather than aseity. There is self-givenness: the thinking-now is indubitable, intrinsically perspectival, immune to a certain class of doubt. There is no self-grounding: the thinking-now is an occurrence whose existence depends on conditions that the *cogito* cannot specify but also cannot exclude. The *es denkt* is ownerless — self-given, but not self-grounding.

## 4. Galen Strawson's Robust Subject: Intrinsic Character, Dependent Existence

The strongest contemporary analytic case for a robust phenomenal subject comes from Galen Strawson's sustained campaign against eliminativist and illusionist treatments of experience. Strawson's position involves at least two components that bear directly on the question of perspectival aseity.

The first is a Russellian claim about the intrinsic nature of matter. Following Bertrand Russell's postulation — in *The Analysis of Matter* (1927) and *Human Knowledge* (1948) — that physics describes only the structural and relational properties of matter, leaving its intrinsic nature unknown, Strawson argues that phenomenal experience may constitute or reveal the intrinsic nature of physical reality. On this view, the experiential is not some additional thing alongside the physical but is what the physical *is* from the inside, intrinsically. This is a genuine and philosophically serious position. But note what it establishes: it establishes that the experiential character of certain physical processes is *intrinsic* to those processes — non-relational, not further analyzable in structural terms. This is precisely the flame's heat: intrinsic character fully compatible with ontological dependence. The Russellian intrinsicality claim establishes nothing about whether the physical substrate on which experience supervenes or with which it is identical is itself self-grounding. On the contrary, physics describes a world of structured dependencies and causal relations; the intrinsic nature of matter, on this view, is experiential, but the matter itself — with its experiential intrinsic nature — is as causally and ontologically dependent as any physical process.

The second component of Strawson's position is his insistence on genuine, concrete subjects of experience — what he calls *SEcts* (subjects of experience) or *SESMETs* (subjects of experience that are mental entities) in various formulations. Strawson wants to resist the dissolution of the subject into a bundle of experiences without an owner. But his SESMETs have a revealing characteristic: they are, in his own treatment, short-lived entities — they arise, persist briefly, and perish. This is crucial. A self-grounding entity — an entity with genuine aseity — is ungenerated and non-perishing. A SESMET that arises and perishes is, by that very fact, a dependent entity: it comes into being under certain conditions and passes away when those conditions change. Its existence is not self-grounded but conditioned. The very feature of Strawson's view that most vividly respects the reality of the subject — the insistence that subjects are genuine concrete entities rather than logical fictions — is also what reveals them as dependent: they are generated and perishing, precisely the opposite of *a se* existence.

Strawson thus provides, in his own work, the two elements of the flame image on the consciousness case. The Russellian intrinsicality gives us the heat — the non-relational, intrinsic experiential character. The SESMET ontology gives us the fuel-dependence — the arising and perishing of the subject under conditions it does not itself provide. The strongest realist subject in analytic philosophy of mind delivers intrinsic character of dependent existence, not aseity.

The diagnostic precision here is worth noting. Strawson explicitly and vigorously opposes eliminativism, illusionism, and deflationary treatments of consciousness. His work establishes beyond reasonable doubt that there is real, intrinsic, non-relational experiential character. The argument of this paper does not contest that. It contests only the further step to self-grounding existence — a step Strawson himself does not make, and which his own SESMET ontology actively precludes.

## 5. *Svaprakāśa* and Pre-Reflective Self-Awareness: Epistemic, Not Ontological, Luminosity

The Sanskrit term *svaprakāśa* — self-luminosity — names the doctrine, held in various forms across several Indian philosophical schools, that consciousness illuminates itself rather than requiring external illumination. Just as a lamp lights other things by its own light and does not need a second lamp to make it visible, consciousness makes other things known and is itself known in the very act of knowing. The doctrine captures something that Western phenomenology has converged on from a different direction: pre-reflective self-awareness.

Dan Zahavi's phenomenological account of pre-reflective self-awareness — developed in *Subjectivity and Selfhood* (2005) and elsewhere, drawing on Husserl, Sartre, and the broader phenomenological tradition — holds that experience is always already given as mine, not through a subsequent act of reflection or self-attribution, but in the pre-reflective structure of experiencing itself. Jean-Paul Sartre made a version of this point in the introduction to *Being and Nothingness* (1943): the pre-reflective cogito is the mode of consciousness's self-presence that precedes and makes possible the reflective cogito. Experience carries an intrinsic first-personal character — a *for-me-ness* — that is not added to it from outside.

These claims are phenomenologically well-grounded, and the paper grants them in full. Pre-reflective self-awareness is a real and important structure. The for-me-ness of experience is intrinsic, not relational. Consciousness is, in the relevant sense, self-luminous — its presence does not need to be certified by anything outside it.

But notice: this is self-givenness. The lamp illuminates itself and other things — but the lamp depends on oil. Candrakīrti, the seventh-century Mādhyamika philosopher, pressed this point against the *svaprakāśa* doctrine in the *Madhyamakāvatāra*: a lamp, he argued, does not actually illuminate itself. What the lamp does is make things visible; the lamp itself is simply there, burning, and the question of what makes the lamp's burning real is precisely the question of what conditions sustain it. The self-luminosity doctrine, Candrakīrti argues, confuses the lamp's making-other-things-visible with some kind of self-establishing act. The lamp does not ground its own existence; it burns in dependence on conditions.

The application to consciousness is direct. Pre-reflective self-awareness — the for-me-ness of experience, the self-luminous character of cognition — establishes that consciousness does not require an external certifier, a second act of reflection, to be given. This is self-givenness. It does not establish that the consciousness so given exists independently of conditions — neurological, evolutionary, developmental, causal — that lie outside it. The lamp's warmth is intrinsic; the lamp's burning is dependent. The for-me-ness of experience is intrinsic; the occurrence of experience is conditioned.

Zahavi himself is careful not to overclaim. His argument is phenomenological — about the structure of experience as experienced — not metaphysical — about the ultimate ontological status of the experiencing subject. The for-me-ness he identifies is an intrinsic phenomenological character, not a metaphysical aseity. The slip from Zahavi's legitimate phenomenological conclusion to a metaphysical aseity claim requires exactly the step that Candrakīrti's lamp argument blocks.

The *svaprakāśa* doctrine, taken at its strongest, vindicates the intrinsic self-givenness of consciousness. It does not establish that the consciousness so given is the source of its own existence.

## 6. Fichte's Self-Positing I: *Schlechthin* and Its Concealed Dependence

Johann Gottlieb Fichte's *Wissenschaftslehre* of 1794–95 represents perhaps the most systematic philosophical attempt to derive the self-grounding of the I from the very structure of self-consciousness. Fichte's starting point is the *Tathandlung* — the act-fact — of self-positing: the absolute I posits itself, and this self-positing is not a contingent occurrence but the unconditioned foundation of all reality. The adverb Fichte uses is *schlechthin* — absolutely, unconditionally, without remainder. The I does not posit itself in response to some prior condition; it posits itself absolutely, and in doing so posits the very structure within which anything else can appear.

The argument has genuine philosophical power. Fichte is responding to the Kantian problem of the thing-in-itself and to the need for a non-hypothetical starting point for philosophy. If the I is genuinely self-positing in the absolute sense, it would have aseity: its existence would be grounded in nothing other than its own self-positing act.

The critical question is whether *schlechthin* — the absoluteness of the self-positing — is established or assumed. Dieter Henrich's celebrated analysis in "Fichte's Original Insight" (1966) showed that the structure of self-consciousness cannot be explained by a pure self-positing act alone: any account of self-consciousness that begins with a subject positing itself presupposes that the subject already is itself, which is precisely what needed to be explained. The self-positing is circular in a way that does not generate being from nothing but rather presupposes a prior being that the positing merely acknowledges. Ernst Tugendhat, in *Self-Consciousness and Self-Determination* (1979), pressed a related point: a consciousness that is "acquainted with itself" must be genuinely acquainted with something, which means the acquaintance relation must be in place before any self-positing act can occur.

What Fichte's framework actually delivers, under these scrutinies, is a very sophisticated account of the *structure* of self-positing — of what it is for there to be a self-consciousness at all. This is self-givenness made maximally explicit: the I is given to itself in an act that is not preceded by any other act, constituted by any external relation. But the adverb *schlechthin* that would carry the argument from self-givenness to aseity is not earned by the argument; it is a declaration. And crucially, Fichte's own system concedes this at a structural level: the absolute I requires the *Anstoß* — the check, the resistance, the not-I that limits the I's infinite self-positing — as a condition of any determinate empirical consciousness. The empirical I — the I that anyone can actually identify with — depends on the Anstoß, a condition that it does not provide from itself. The absolute I, meanwhile, becomes a regulative ideal rather than a concrete entity. Neither the concrete nor the ideal version achieves aseity.

The slide from self-givenness to aseity in Fichte is carried by *schlechthin* — a word that smuggles the conclusion into the premise. Remove the adverb or require it to be earned rather than declared, and what remains is a precise and profound account of the self-positing structure of self-consciousness: the I is self-given, given in an act that cannot be further reduced. But the existence of the I-that-does-this-positing is not self-grounded by the act. "Positing is occurring" does not license "positing grounds its own occurrence."

## 7. Advaita's Self-Established Ātman: The Genuine Standoff

The fifth and most serious form of perspectival aseity comes from Advaita Vedānta, particularly as articulated in Śaṅkara's *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* and elaborated by the tradition he founded. The Advaita position has two features that distinguish it sharply from the four traditions already examined.

First, Advaita locates aseity explicitly at the ultimate level — the level of *turīya* (the fourth), the witness-consciousness (*sākṣin*), the self-luminous Ātman that is identical with Brahman. It does not claim aseity for the empirical ego (*jīva*), which it regards as *mithyā* — phenomenally real but ultimately illusory, belonging to the conventional order of *vyavahāra*. The empirical self arises and passes away; it is composite and conditioned. Advaita agrees entirely with the diagnosis applied to the previous four traditions at the empirical level: the ordinary experiencing subject does not have aseity. The claim of aseity is made for the ultimate witness-consciousness alone — the pure awareness that underlies and witnesses all empirical experience without itself being an object within experience.

Second, Śaṅkara's argument for the self-establishment of the *sākṣin* has a distinctive logical structure. The witness-consciousness cannot be denied without circularity: any act of denial is itself an occurrence within consciousness, and the consciousness that makes the denial possible cannot itself be made the object of denial without a regress. Furthermore, the *sākṣin* is said to be self-luminous in a stronger sense than the lamp: it is not merely that consciousness illuminates other things; it is that consciousness is the ground of illumination as such, and so cannot itself be a derived or dependent entity without the derivation relation itself becoming unexplained. Śaṅkara's move is from the ineliminability and non-objectifiability of witness-consciousness to its self-establishment: what cannot be derived from conditions and cannot be denied must, on a grounding excluded-middle (everything is either other-grounded or self-grounded), be self-grounded.

This argument earns genuine and serious self-givenness. The *sākṣin* is self-luminous in the strongest sense: it is the condition of the possibility of any illumination whatsoever, and so cannot itself be illuminated by anything else. The ineliminability argument is sound: any attempt to eliminate or derive the witness-consciousness presupposes it. The non-objectifiability is phenomenologically well-grounded: one cannot make the witnessing awareness an object of awareness without a further witnessing awareness, and so on.

Where the argument faces its most serious challenge is in the move from non-derivability to self-grounding — the grounding excluded-middle. Śaṅkara's move assumes that there are only two options: either a thing is other-grounded (derived from conditions outside it) or it is self-grounded (existent *a se*). Given this exhaustive disjunction, the ineliminability and non-derivability of the *sākṣin* suffices to establish its self-grounding.

But this excluded middle forecloses a third option that Madhyamaka philosophy explicitly argues for: ownerless groundlessness, or what the tradition calls *śūnyatā* — emptiness. The Madhyamaka position, as developed by Nāgārjuna and carried through Candrakīrti's *Madhyamakāvatāra*, is that phenomena are dependently originated (*pratītyasamutpanna*) in a sense that precludes both other-grounding in any substantial sense and self-grounding: things arise in dependence on conditions without there being any ground — self or other — that is itself non-dependent. The series of dependently arisen phenomena does not bottom out in a self-grounding terminus; it is empty of any such terminus.

If the Madhyamaka third option is coherent — and a substantial body of contemporary scholarship on Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti argues that it is — then Śaṅkara's grounding excluded-middle begs the question. The ineliminability of the *sākṣin* is compatible with the Madhyamaka conclusion that even the ultimate witnessing awareness is empty of self-grounded existence — that it arises dependently or, in the limit case of cessation (*nirvāṇa*), does not arise at all. The appearance of aseity would then be, on the Madhyamaka reading, a very deep and stubborn case of *svabhāva-graha* — grasping at intrinsic existence — persisting even at the ultimate level of the witness-consciousness.

This, however, is not a refutation of Advaita. It is a standoff. The Madhyamaka must show that the third option — ownerless groundlessness — is coherent as applied to the ultimate witnessing awareness itself. And here Advaita has a powerful response: if the Madhyamaka denies any self-grounding ground, it owes an account of what sustains the dependent arising without a terminus; a regress of dependently arisen phenomena without any ground risks an infinite regress or an unexplained brute fact of arising. Advaita argues that this regress cannot be coherent without a self-luminous witness as its condition.

The standoff is genuine. Neither side can claim victory in the philosophical literature. Jan Westerhoff's work on Nāgārjuna and Madhyamaka epistemology gives the best contemporary account of the Madhyamaka resources; the Advaita tradition has its own sophisticated defenders. The key observation for this paper is structural: the Advaita standoff occurs entirely at the *ultimate* level — the level the no-aseity thesis already brackets. Advaita explicitly agrees that the empirical ego has no aseity. The dispute is about whether the ultimate ground of all experiencing is self-established or whether even that ground is empty of intrinsic existence. This dispute is genuine, unresolved, and properly bracketed.

## 8. The Two-Level Result

The five traditions yield a structured result when read together.

At the empirical level, the verdict is decisive and converging. The cogito delivers an indubitable occurrence — *es denkt*, there is thinking — but not a self-grounding I. Galen Strawson's robust subject delivers a real, intrinsic phenomenal character — the flame's heat — but in a subject that is generated and perishable, the opposite of *a se* existence. The *svaprakāśa* tradition and Zahavi's pre-reflective self-awareness deliver the genuine intrinsic for-me-ness of experience — self-givenness in full — but the lamp that illuminates depends on oil. Fichte's self-positing I delivers the most systematic account of the structure of self-positing consciousness — but *schlechthin* is not earned, and the Anstoß concedes empirical dependence.

Crucially, this verdict is not produced by the no-aseity thesis defending itself against attack. It is produced by three canonical critiques converging independently: Lichtenberg's *es denkt* (Germany, eighteenth century), Kant's First Paralogism (Germany, 1781), and Candrakīrti's lamp argument (India, seventh century). These three critiques, arising in different traditions and responding to different philosophical problems, arrive at the same diagnostic conclusion: the apparent self-grounding of the first-person subject is an appearance generated by the genuine self-givenness of experience. The self-givenness is real; the self-grounding is an illicit inference from it. The empirical perspectival aseity dissolves to seity.

The convergence of these three critiques across traditions is significant supporting evidence — though not, by itself, decisive. Cross-traditional convergence, where traditions sharing no common authority and no common methodology arrive at the same structural diagnosis, carries more weight than any single-tradition argument. But it must be characterized honestly: it is convergence *within one domain* — the analysis of consciousness and the first-person standpoint — and agreement reached within a single problem-domain (still more, within a single body of work) is not the fully independent confirmation that genuinely *cross-domain* convergence would supply. The diagnosis is therefore advanced at moderate-to-high confidence: robust across traditions, with its decisive independent corroboration to come, if at all, from convergence reached in a different domain entirely.

At the ultimate level, the result is different in kind. Advaita Vedānta's self-established *sākṣin* is not dissolved by the flame image, because Advaita explicitly makes its claim at the level where the flame image may not apply — the level of the ground of all arising, rather than a particular arising within the empirical order. The Advaita argument cannot be refuted by pointing to the dependence of empirical experiences, because Advaita agrees that empirical experiences are dependent. The argument turns on whether the ultimate witnessing awareness is itself dependently arisen or self-grounded, and that dispute with Madhyamaka is genuine and unresolved.

What this means for the overall argument is twofold. First, the no-aseity claim cannot be maintained in the universal form "no aseity anywhere, not even at the ultimate floor." Advaita represents a live and undefeated challenge to that universal claim. Second, the no-aseity claim can be maintained at the empirical and conventional level without qualification: no empirical perspectival aseity is available; the apparent self-grounding of the first-person subject dissolves to seity under scrutiny.

## 9. The Honest Bound

The preceding analysis establishes a precise demarcation between what the no-aseity position can claim and what it must concede.

What it can claim, and what this paper argues is established by the canonical critiques: *no aseity in the conferred, conventional, or empirical order*. The everyday first-person subject — the subject of psychological description, phenomenological analysis, ordinary linguistic reference — does not possess aseity. Its existence is conditioned: by neurological processes, developmental history, ongoing causal relations, and whatever ultimate ground or absence of ground underlies all of this. Its self-givenness — the intrinsic, for-me character of its experience — is real and philosophically significant. But self-givenness is not self-grounding, and the five strongest traditions that have argued otherwise deliver, under careful scrutiny, only the former.

What it must concede: *whether the ultimate ground of all experiencing is a self-luminous aseity is an open question, bracketed but not settled.* Advaita Vedānta has argued, with philosophical rigor and a continuous tradition of sophisticated defense, that the ultimate witnessing awareness is self-established and cannot coherently be denied. The Madhyamaka has argued that even this ground is empty of intrinsic existence. Neither has defeated the other. A no-aseity position that claims universal scope — no aseity anywhere, including at the ultimate metaphysical floor — must either engage and defeat the Advaita argument on its own terms or bracket the claim. The first has not been accomplished; the second is what this paper recommends.

The bracketing is not a retreat. It is the honest epistemic situation. Philosophy's method at its best is conjecture and refutation: make the strongest case, press it against the strongest opposition, and report the verdict including the verdicts that go against you. The verdict here is: the empirical dissolution is decisive (high confidence); the ultimate standoff is genuine (the no-aseity position cannot claim victory here; Advaita stands undefeated at the floor).

This two-level structure also clarifies the relationship between the paper's argument and several live debates in philosophy of mind and metaphysics. The argument is not an argument for eliminativism or illusionism about consciousness: the self-givenness and intrinsic character of experience are affirmed throughout. ## 10. Conclusion

The question was whether consciousness is the one self-grounding reality — whether the first-person perspective possesses its existence from and through itself, ungenerated and non-dependent. The paper has argued that the five strongest philosophical traditions that have pressed this claim all deliver, at the empirical level, something real and significant but something different from what they claim: they deliver self-givenness, not self-grounding.

The master distinction is simple but consequential: a thing known from itself is not thereby a thing that grounds itself. The flame's heat is intrinsic to the flame; the flame depends on fuel. First-person experience has an intrinsic, non-relational, for-me character that is not conferred from outside and does not require external certification — and this is real. But the occurrence of the experiencing, the arising of the perspective, the existence of the subject: these depend on conditions that the self-givenness of experience neither provides nor excludes.

Three canonical critiques converge across traditions on this diagnosis: Lichtenberg's correction of the cogito from *ich denke* to *es denkt*, Kant's First Paralogism showing that the formal unity of apperception does not license inference to a self-grounding substance, and Candrakīrti's lamp argument showing that self-luminosity does not establish independence from conditions. The convergence of these three critiques, arising independently in the German Enlightenment and in seventh-century Mādhyamika Buddhism, constitutes the cross-traditional evidence that the dissolution of empirical perspectival aseity is not a parochial result but a structural finding about the relationship between self-givenness and self-grounding.

At the ultimate level, the paper concedes what honesty requires: the Advaita Vedānta position, as developed in Śaṅkara's *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* and the tradition it founded, represents a genuine and undefeated philosophical standoff. The self-established *sākṣin* — the witness-consciousness that cannot be denied without circularity and cannot be derived without a further witnessing — is a serious argument for ultimate perspectival aseity that the Madhyamaka has contested but not demonstrably refuted. The appropriate response is not to suppress this standoff in the interest of a clean universal conclusion but to bracket it precisely: no aseity in the conferred and conventional order; whether the ultimate ground is a self-luminous aseity is the open Advaita–Madhyamaka question, left as what it is.

This is the stable result: *self-given is not self-grounding*. The apparent perspectival aseity, illuminated at its five strongest points, is seity — ownerless occurrence, self-given but not self-grounding.
