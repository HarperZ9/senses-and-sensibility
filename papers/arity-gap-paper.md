# Sourcehood Without a Source: The Arity Gap and the Free Will Debate

**Abstract**

The free will debate has been conducted, for the most part, as a search for an origin — a point at which the agent, rather than the agent's antecedents, is responsible for what comes next. Libertarians locate that point in an undetermined act of will; hard incompatibilists argue, with Galen Strawson, that no such point exists because nothing can be *causa sui*; compatibilists relocate it into the agent's psychology and hope the relocation does enough work. What unites all three parties is the aseity premise: the assumption that free will, if it exists, consists in a form of self-origination. This paper argues that the aseity premise is not a discovery about what free will requires but a definitional smuggling that makes the debate unsolvable from the start. Once the premise is dropped, the question is not answered so much as reframed: sourcehood — being the genuine author of one's acts — is either impossible or it is *conferred*, inhabited rather than secreted, addressed rather than self-caused. The paper then identifies what it takes to be the free will debate's single most load-bearing structural move: the **arity gap**. P.F. Strawson's second-personal account of the reactive attitudes, read with precision rather than in its standard quietist register, reveals that holding-responsible is a two-place relation — between an addresser and an addressee, each standing at a second-personal pole — and that the hard incompatibilist's proposed reduction of that relation to a first-person-to-third-person program of behavior-shaping is blocked not by intuition or pragmatics but by logical structure. A one-place, target-fixing vocabulary cannot fix the target of a two-place relation. The phlogiston elimination succeeds for combustion because oxidation is a one-place property of the combusting stuff. It cannot succeed for holding-responsible because the second person is not a predicate added to a property but the second argument-place of the relation itself. Determinism is granted in full throughout; the chaos-equivocation is explicitly withdrawn. The conclusion is that conferred sourcehood is stronger than libertarianism, not weaker: it never incurred the debt libertarianism cannot pay.

**Keywords:** free will, reactive attitudes, P.F. Strawson, arity gap, second-personal standpoint, Galen Strawson Basic Argument, conferred sourcehood, compatibilism, hard incompatibilism, Pereboom, Sapolsky

## 1. The Aseity Premise and Why It Should Not Have Been There

Every major position in the free will debate carries, embedded in its motivating picture, a demand that almost no one has made fully explicit: the demand that the free agent be, in some non-trivial sense, the *source of the source* of its own acts. Libertarians make this explicit and try to satisfy it — through agent causation, through undetermined self-forming actions (Kane 1996), through some account of how the agent's undetermined choice is nonetheless the *agent's* rather than mere noise. Hard incompatibilists, led by Galen Strawson's Basic Argument (1994, *Philosophical Studies*; high confidence on text and thesis), make the demand explicit and demonstrate that nothing can satisfy it, concluding that no one is ever truly morally responsible. Compatibilists attempt to defuse the demand by arguing that "could have done otherwise," properly understood, does not require self-origination — but most compatibilist accounts, at their deepest joint, reinstall something that functions like a self-standing authoritative self that halts the regress of endorsement. The aseity premise runs through the debate like a ground current, and the debate runs on it.

The premise, stripped of clothing, is this: an act is free, and the agent is genuinely responsible for it, only if the agent is ultimately the origin of the principles, values, and character from which the act flows — and is responsible for being that way, and so on, all the way down. This is Galen Strawson's formalization, and it is, as he says, a regress that terminates only in the impossible: *causa sui*, the self-caused self, which Nietzsche correctly called the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far (*Beyond Good and Evil* §21; Galen Strawson cites this passage in developing the Basic Argument; high confidence on the provenance). Nothing is *causa sui*. The chain of causes runs through the agent and does not stop there, and the agent did not write the initial conditions.

Here is what this paper proposes: that is all correct, and it settles exactly the wrong question. The argument from premises 1 through 5 of the Basic Argument is sound. What follows from it is that no one is the *ultimate, self-originating* source of their acts. What does not follow from it — what the argument can establish only by carrying the aseity premise forward into the conclusion as an unargued definitional stipulation — is that there is therefore no *sourcehood* of any kind, no responsibility, no free will worth wanting. That inference is valid only if responsibility and sourcehood *mean* ultimate self-origination. They need not mean that. The aseity demand is embedded in the words "true" and "ultimate" as Galen Strawson uses them, and the embedding is doing all the work in the step from the sound premises to the nihilist conclusion. Strip the demand and the premises establish exactly what they establish — the will is not *causa sui* — without establishing anything about whether conferred, inhabited, second-personally-constituted sourcehood is real.

This is not a deflationary compatibilist move of the kind that simply lowers the bar for what counts as free will and declares the game won. The claim is structural, not redefinitional: *aseity was never the right criterion*, not merely a criterion that happens to be unmet. The grounds for that structural claim emerge from cross-traditional evidence and from the arity argument developed in Section 4 below. For now, observe only that the aseity demand was always optional: it is not analytically contained in the concept of responsibility, it is not entailed by the phenomenology of deliberation, and as several philosophical traditions that never granted the will aseity demonstrate, it is possible to hold agents fully responsible and to understand agency as fully real while treating sourcehood as *conferred* rather than originated.

## 2. Determinism, Granted in Full, Is Orthogonal

This paper grants the consequence argument (van Inwagen 1983, *An Essay on Free Will*; high confidence): if determinism is true, our acts are the consequences of the laws of nature and the remote past, over neither of which we have or ever had any choice; therefore we have no choice about our acts. The argument is sound given its premises, and those premises are not here contested. Determinism is taken as given throughout.

The point is not that determinism is false. The point is that the consequence argument, correctly read, is itself a no-aseity argument: it shows that the agent is not the ultimate source, that the past and the laws write the future. What the consequence argument does not show — what it cannot show — is that there is no sourcehood other than ultimate self-origination. It removes *leeway* (the could-have-done-otherwise alternatives). It does not touch *sourcehood* — the question of whether the act that actually occurred flows through the agent as a genuine source or bypasses the agent as an efficient cause flows through inert matter. Those are different questions, and Frankfurt's cases (1969, *Journal of Philosophy*; high confidence), whatever their ultimate success against Principle of Alternate Possibilities, got the distinction right: what matters for responsibility is not that the agent could have done otherwise but that the act in the actual sequence ran through the agent's own agency. Sourcehood is a question about the actual sequence, not about alternatives.

A separate matter deserves explicit burial: the appeal to chaos theory and indeterminism as any kind of support for free will. The corpus from which this paper grows originally used the Lorenz attractor and sensitive dependence on initial conditions to give the orthogonality thesis "mathematical teeth." That claim equivocates between epistemic unpredictability and constitutive irreducibility, and it is here withdrawn. A determined-and-irreducible trajectory in a chaotic system is not, by being irreducible, a *source*. That a hurricane's path cannot be predicted from its micro-initial conditions is an epistemic and computational fact about observers; it is not the fact that the hurricane authored anything. Unpredictability is not authorship. The chaos argument is withdrawn, not softened.

What the chaos mathematics *does* legitimately establish is narrower and more modest: it shows that the inference from determinism to *eliminative reduction* — from "the act is determined" to "the act dissolves into its substrate and can be fully described at the micro-level" — is invalid. Deterministic systems can be computationally irreducible (Wolfram; moderate confidence on attribution, high confidence on the phenomenon), which means the macro-level description is not eliminable in favor of the micro-level description even under full determinism. This vacates the hard determinist's *reductive* premise without supplying any positive content to sourcehood. The positive content comes from elsewhere — from the argument of Sections 4 through 6. The chaos result clears the space the reductionist claimed was already occupied; it does not fill that space with sourcehood. That distinction is precise and the paper holds to it throughout.

## 3. Three Traditions That Never Owed the Debt

Before developing the arity argument, it is worth registering that conferred sourcehood is not a philosopher's escape hatch invented to dodge a dilemma produced by Western assumptions. The structure of ownership-without-origination has been independently arrived at in multiple traditions that faced the freedom-under-total-determination problem without ever granting the aseity premise.

**Ash'arī kasb.** Classical Ash'arī kalām — the doctrine elaborated from al-Ash'arī (d. 935) through al-Bāqillānī, al-Juwaynī, and al-Ghazālī (high confidence on the existence and core of the doctrine; moderate confidence on the finer scholastic disputes over the mechanism of *kasb*, which were contested within the school) — holds that God creates (*khalq*) every act and the human being *acquires* (*iktisāb*) it. God is the only true cause; the human is not the ontological originator of the act. Yet the act is *the agent's* for purposes of responsibility precisely because of the acquisition — the inhabiting of the act from the standpoint of the person whose act it is. This is conferred sourcehood in its strict form: total causal dependence with genuine ownership at the locus, achieved without any self-origination on the agent's part. The Mu'tazila pressed for real causal power; the *falāsifa* charged that *kasb* is a distinction without a difference. The Ash'arī position is the refusal of both horns — ownership without origination — arrived at seven centuries before the compatibilism/incompatibilism terminology was coined.

**Yoruba *orì*.** In Yoruba thought (Wande Abimbola; Segun Gbadegesin, *African Philosophy*, 1991; Barry Hallen; moderate confidence on the philosophical interpretation, which varies by source; high confidence on the broad structure), *orì* — the inner head, the bearer of personal destiny — is received before birth and lived out. The destiny is conferred; the lived life is its habitation; *ìwà* (character) and effort remain real and answerable within the destiny conferred. This structure is arrived at entirely outside the Abrahamic frame and without any reference to the Western debate's terms. The convergence is not explicable by common inheritance.

**The tied camel.** The Prophetic hadith transmitted in al-Tirmidhī (*iʿqil baʿīrak wa tawakkal*; moderate confidence on the exact chain/grading; high confidence that the saying is canonical and widely transmitted in this sense) — tie your camel and then rely on God — does not say: since all is decreed, do nothing. It says: act fully, with your own hands, exhaustively, as if it were entirely up to you, and within that very acting, the decree is operative. The acting is not in competition with the decree; it is *how* one inhabits a conferred standpoint inside a determined order.

Three traditions, no shared metaphysics, each facing freedom under total determination, each arriving independently at ownership-without-origination rather than either the libertarian demand or the hard-incompatibilist dissolution. The Western debate's peculiarity is that it built the aseity demand into the *definition* of free will and then discovered, with Galen Strawson and Robert Sapolsky, that nothing can meet it — concluding "no free will" rather than "wrong definition." The witnesses show the definition was always optional. Conferred sourcehood is stronger than libertarianism, not weaker, because it never incurred the debt libertarianism cannot pay: it never required the agent to be *causa sui*.

## 4. The Arity Gap: The Core Argument

### 4.1 P.F. Strawson's Move, Read Precisely

P.F. Strawson, in "Freedom and Resentment" (1962, *Proceedings of the British Academy*; high confidence on text and thesis), made a move whose radicalism remains underweighted in most receptions of it. The traditional question — does determinism render the reactive attitudes unjustified? — he declined to answer on its own terms. He argued instead that the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, hurt feelings, forgiveness — the whole register of responses to the quality of will another holds toward us) are not conclusions drawn from a prior theoretical belief about causation. They are constitutive of participation in interpersonal life. The "objective attitude" — viewing another as an object to be managed, treated, cured, or trained rather than addressed — is available episodically (toward the very young, the severely psychotic, in moments of strategic detachment), but the commitment to interpersonal relationship that grounds the reactive attitudes is not the kind of thing a metaphysical thesis can dislodge.

The standard reading takes this as a naturalized, near-quietist compatibilism: responsibility just *is* the practice of holding-responsible, and the practice is immune to metaphysical revision. This reading, while not exactly wrong, fails to capture what makes the move structurally interesting — and it is exactly the reading that makes Strawson look refutable to the hard incompatibilist, because the quietist version seems to be simply changing the subject rather than answering the question. The precise reading, developed with rigor by Stephen Darwall (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006; high confidence), is this: the reactive attitudes encode a *second-personal* relation. To hold you responsible is to address a demand to you, from within a relationship of mutual accountability, presupposing your standing to address me in turn. The "you" in "I hold *you* responsible" is not a designation of an object in the third person; it is the second relatum of an address. This is not the quietist point that practices are immune to revision. It is a structural claim about the logical form of holding-responsible.

### 4.2 The Hard Incompatibilist's Best Move

Robert Sapolsky (*Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will*, 2023; high confidence on the book's thesis) and Derk Pereboom (*Living Without Free Will*, 2001; *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*, 2014; high confidence on both) argue that once the full causal history of any act is traced — through neurons, hormones, development, culture, evolution — there is no remaining room for desert, and therefore no warrant for the backward-looking reactive attitudes. Their positive proposal is the forward-looking package: retain the *protective* functions — quarantine of the dangerous on the public-health model, deterrence, moral formation, reconciliation — and discard basic desert. On this picture, "I hold you responsible" is a folk-psychological wrapper around a behavior-shaping signal. The resentment is the affective costume of the signal. Reduce the costume and nothing of normative value is lost; and something is gained, because the costume licenses cruelty.

This is a serious proposal and it deserves a serious reply. The reply here is not that the practice is sacrosanct, or that metaphysics cannot touch it, or that the folk psychology is self-evidently correct. The reply is structural: **the reduction fails because it equivocates on arity**.

### 4.3 The Arity Argument, Stated Precisely

A forward-looking behavior-shaping relation is, in its logical form, **first-person to third-person**: an agent (me, the state, the institution) acts *upon* a system (you, modeled as a bundle of modifiable dispositions) to alter its future outputs. The relation is fully specified without any address — without the moment in which I treat you as a *source who could answer me*, who has standing to say "you have wronged me" back, whose acknowledgment or refusal of my demand is itself the point and not merely a lever for the next adjustment.

The reactive attitudes, by contrast, are not affective decorations on a control signal. They are the *registration of the other as an addressable source*. When I resent you, I am not merely predicting and steering your future behavior — I may resent you precisely when steering is hopeless and prediction certain. I am holding open a relation in which your *will toward me* is the object of response. Forgiveness makes this vivid: to forgive is not to cease a deterrence program. One can deter without forgiving and forgive without ceasing to deter. To forgive is to release a *claim* that presupposed you were a source against whom a claim could lie.

The hard incompatibilist's proposal to eliminate the reactive attitudes in favor of the forward-looking package is therefore not a reduction of one thing to another. It is the replacement of a **two-place relation** (addresser and addressee, each occupying a second-personal pole) with a **one-place predicate** (this agent has a disposition-profile that can be modified by these interventions). The second person is not a predicate added to a third-person property; it is the *second argument-place of the relation itself*. Eliminate it and you have not removed a gloss from something more fundamental. You have removed one of the two relata and are now describing something structurally different.

This is the arity gap: **a one-place, target-fixing vocabulary cannot fix the target of a two-place relation**.

### 4.4 The Phlogiston Objection and Its Failure

The most searching version of the hard incompatibilist objection is the phlogiston analogy. It runs like this: I am not simply replacing the reactive attitudes with something else. I am identifying what the reactive attitudes were *tracking* — behavior-shaping signals — and eliminating only the desert-laden wrapper, keeping the function. This is precisely how phlogiston elimination works: it identifies what phlogiston-talk was tracking (combustion) and replaces the theory while retaining the phenomenon. I am doing the same. Your "second-personal remainder" is just the name you give to your refusal to finish the reduction.

This objection is the best in the literature. It must be answered on its own terms, not evaded.

The answer is that the phlogiston analogy contains its own refutation. Phlogiston elimination succeeds because there is a *target-fixing description* of combustion — oxidation — that is available in the third person, that captures everything phlogiston-talk was tracking, and against which the old theoretical gloss can be measured and found empty. Oxidation is a one-place property of the combusting stuff. The proposed elimination of the reactive attitudes owes the same: a third-personal, forward-looking, target-fixing description of what holding-responsible was tracking, against which the second-personal "gloss" can be measured and found to add nothing. **The hard incompatibilist does not have this description, and the reason is structural rather than a gap in current science.**

Map-coloring reduces. Combustion reduces. Geocentric apparent motion reduces. All of these are one-place or already third-personal phenomena whose target-fixing description is available without reference to the second person. The second-personal relation is the one case the move cannot reach, because its target — the addressee *qua addressee*, the other as a source who can answer — is constitutively part of what is being tracked, not a theoretical commitment that can be stripped while the phenomenon is retained.

The hard incompatibilist has a reduction-plus-elimination *schema*. He does not have an *instance* of it for the two-place case. And the two-place-ness of holding-responsible is not incidental to it. It is what holding-responsible *is*.

### 4.5 The Solipsism Test: Proof of Floating Free

The claim that the second-personal remainder (R) is something over and above what any monadic account of agency can supply — that it does not merely redescribe the necessity of an inhabited standpoint — requires a precise demonstration, not merely an assertion. The test is this:

Consider a solipsistic universe containing exactly one inhabited standpoint. In this universe, the necessity-of-locus claim is satisfied in full: there is a standpoint, agency happens there, the inhabited locus is the only place it could occur. But (R) — the second-personal remainder — is *false* in this universe, because there is no one to address. There is no second pole. There is no addressee whose uptake is a success condition for anything. Forgiveness, resentment, and the whole register of second-personal relations have no objects and cannot occur.

A proposition that can be *false* in a universe where the necessity-of-locus claim is *true* is not entailed by that claim. It says something strictly additional. The arity gap — the gap between the monadic necessity of a standpoint and the dyadic structure of second-personal address — is a structural gap, not a verbal one. (R) floats free of the residue.

### 4.6 Answerability Without Basic Desert

The arity argument, as stated through §4.5, establishes that holding-responsible is an irreducibly two-place relation and that no monadic, forward-looking vocabulary can target-fix its addressee-pole. What the argument has not yet established — and what Galen Strawson's Basic Argument directly challenges — is whether either pole of that dyadic relation carries *basic desert*: the warranted backward-looking attribution of blame or credit grounded in the agent's own self-authored character. The gap is precise. Proving that the relation is dyadic is not yet proving that the addressee *deserves*, in the regress-vulnerable sense, the demand directed at them. Until that gap is closed, the Basic Argument bites: even a genuinely second-personal address could be addressed to someone who, by the logic of premises 1 through 5, cannot be ultimately responsible for being the kind of person who merits it. This subsection closes the gap — or rather, reroutes around it by separating the normative work the arity argument actually does from the normative work it has so far been silently borrowing from.

**The partition.** The philosophical literature on moral responsibility has, in recent decades, generated a taxonomy that the arity argument can be integrated with rather than obligated to defeat. Gary Watson, in "Two Faces of Responsibility" (1996), distinguishes *attributability* — the question of whether an act expresses the agent's evaluative commitments and is genuinely the agent's — from *accountability* — the question of whether the agent is appropriately subject to demands, claims, and reactive responses. These come apart. Attributability is a condition on whose act it is; accountability is a condition on who stands within the practice of mutual address. David Shoemaker (2011) refines this into a triad — attributability, answerability, and accountability — where *answerability* concerns the agent's standing to be called to account, to be asked for reasons, to be entered into the dialogue of justification; and accountability concerns full reactive engagement including resentment and indignation. The triad matters here because answerability, as Shoemaker deploys it, does not obviously require that the agent be the ultimate self-originating source of the character from which the act flowed. It requires only that the agent be the kind of thing that can be addressed with a demand for reasons and can respond — that the second-personal address has the right kind of uptake available to it.

Stephen Darwall's account of the *second-personal standpoint* (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006) supplies the underlying mechanism. Second-personal authority — the standing to address a demand — is not derived from the addressee's metaphysical self-origination. It is constituted within the address-community itself: I have authority to demand of you because we stand in a relation of mutual accountability, each recognizing in the other a fellow member of the community of reasons-givers, each entitled to make claims the other has standing to answer. The authority is *relational and conferred*, not grounded in either party's aseity. This is the crucial asymmetry. Galen Strawson's Basic Argument (1994) targets desert grounded in *how one is* traced back to *how one became that way* and ultimately to a self-caused self. But Darwall's second-personal authority does not inherit its normative standing from that chain at all. Its standing is earned in the address-community — the mutual recognition of second-personal standing by participants who have uptake-capacity for demands — not borrowed from the agent's self-authorship of their own character.

**The demand-to-blame inference.** The argument can now be stated as a positive inference rather than merely a partition. When I address a second-personal demand to you — "you wronged me"; "you owe me an account" — the normative standing of that demand does not trace through your causal history to an impossible self-caused origin. It traces through your membership in the community of addressable sources. The demand is warranted not because you originated yourself but because you are the kind of thing that can receive and respond to demands: a being with uptake-capacity, reasons-responsiveness, and standing within the mutual-accountability community that P.F. Strawson (1962) described. The Basic Argument's regress applies to *desert grounded in self-origination*: to be truly responsible for what one does, one must be truly responsible for how one is, and so on to the impossible *causa sui*. It does not apply to *answerability grounded in second-personal standing*, because that standing is not derived from a backward-looking chain through self-authorship. It is constituted in the present structure of address-relations. There is no regress to run: the standing is not justified by a prior fact about the agent's self-causation but by a standing feature of the address-community in which both parties participate.

The inference that drives from second-personal address to warranted blame can then be stated: if you stand in the address-community, if you have uptake-capacity for demands, and if you acted from an evaluative orientation that is yours in the attributability sense Watson specifies, then holding you to account — resenting, demanding, attributing blame — is warranted by those three conditions jointly, none of which requires self-origination. The Basic Argument's premises 1 through 5 remain sound throughout; they establish that you did not cause yourself. They do not establish that the second-personal demand directed at you lacks normative standing, because that standing is not borrowed from self-origination in the first place.

R.J. Wallace (*Responsibility and the Moral Sentiments*, 1994) offers a complementary route through the fairness of holding expectations. On Wallace's account, reactive attitudes are fair when the agent has the general capacities for reflective self-control — the ability to grasp and be moved by moral reasons. Fairness, here, is the operative normative category, and it inherits its standing from the *present relational* fact of capacity-possession, not from the historical fact of self-authorship. The regress does not bite because Wallace's condition is synchronic and relational rather than diachronic and self-grounding.

**The honest residual bound.** The partition and the demand-to-blame inference are defended, not proven. Three limits must be stated with precision.

First, the route through Watson's attributability and Wallace's fairness is cleaner for this argument than the route through Shoemaker's full accountability triad, because Shoemaker explicitly places *resentment* on the accountability side of his taxonomy — a side that carries the backward-looking reactive register most exposed to the desert charge. If the arity argument wishes to travel fully within Shoemaker's framework, it must accept that the accountability face of the triad (resentment, indignation, blame in its full backward-looking mode) may not be fully insulated from the Basic Argument by the answerability partition alone. The safer claim: *answerability* and the *attributability* conditions together warrant the demand-to-account and a range of reactive responses; whether they warrant resentment at the full backward-looking intensity Strawson's §8 invokes is a question the partition leaves open.

Second, the demand-to-blame inference moves from "second-personal standing is constituted in the address-community" to "blame is warranted." This move is defensible — blame directed at an addressable source who acted from their own evaluative orientation, within a community of mutual accountability, is not an arbitrary imposition — but it is not demonstrated that this exhausts what "warranted" blame requires. The hard incompatibilist will press: even granting that the address is structurally appropriate, *proportionality* and *desert* in the full sense require more. The answer is that the arity argument does not need to win that point in full. It needs only to show that the second-personal demand carries normative standing that the Basic Argument cannot dissolve — and that is what the demand-to-blame inference establishes.

Third, the argument applies reflexively to the present paper's own standing. If second-personal authority is constituted in address-communities and cannot be self-bestowed, then the claim here to have resolved the desert gap is itself subject to uptake and refusal by the community of philosophical inquirers. The inference is offered to that community; its standing is conferred by the quality of the argument, not by any authority the paper exercises over the Basic Argument by mere assertion. That reflexive application is not a weakness. It is evidence of consistency: the no-aseity structure runs through the argument itself. High confidence on the partition and the mechanism it relies on; moderate confidence on the completeness of the demand-to-blame inference as a full reply to the desert charge; the honest bound is that the inference is currently unfalsified rather than proven unbiteable.

## 5. The Basic Argument, Granted and Redirected

Galen Strawson's Basic Argument (*Philosophical Studies*, 1994; also *Freedom and Belief*, 1986; high confidence) may be stated as follows:

1. To be truly morally responsible for what one does, one must be truly responsible for *how one is* — for the mental nature, the principles, from which the act flows.
2. To be truly responsible for how one is, one must have brought it about that one is that way.
3. To have brought *that* about, one must already have been a certain way — and have been responsible for *that* prior way.
4. This regresses infinitely; to stop it, one must be *causa sui*, ultimately self-originating.
5. Nothing can be *causa sui*.
6. Therefore no one is ever truly, ultimately morally responsible.

This paper agrees with premises 1 through 5 entirely. The aseity-free framework has already denied, across existence, status, meaning, and agency, that anything is self-standing. To exempt the will alone would be to grant it an exemption nothing else receives. The Basic Argument is the no-aseity thesis applied specifically to the will: self-causation *is* aseity, and aseity is impossible, and the Basic Argument has the proof of impossibility right. Van Inwagen's consequence argument is granted on the same grounds: it too is a no-aseity argument in another register, showing that the agent is not the ultimate source.

The point of disagreement is precise and confined to one inference: from premise 6's true content — *no ultimate, self-originating responsibility* — to the conclusion *no responsibility, no sourcehood of any kind*. That inference is valid only if responsibility and sourcehood mean ultimate self-origination. The aseity demand is embedded in the words "true" and "ultimate" as Galen Strawson uses them. Strip the demand and premises 1 through 5 establish what they establish — the will is not *causa sui* — and establish *nothing* about whether there is conferred, inhabited, second-personally-constituted sourcehood. Conferred sourcehood was never a candidate for self-origination and is therefore untouched by the proof that self-origination is impossible.

The Basic Argument refutes libertarianism, which *did* claim self-origination. It refutes Kane's event-causal libertarianism (*The Significance of Free Will*, 1996; high confidence), which attempted to buy back aseity within a naturalistic frame through undetermined self-forming actions. Even granting Kane the indeterminism, the luck objection (Mele, *Free Will and Luck*, 2006; high confidence) bites: an undetermined choice is, at the moment of undetermination, not *more* the agent's — it is *less* so, because nothing about the agent settles it. This is the deep reason aseity is the wrong place to look: *self-origination, even if secured, would not give ownership*. It would give a coin-flip the agent happens to be standing next to. Ownership is conferred and inhabited, not originated. Kane's heroic effort to secure aseity, and its vulnerability to luck, is perhaps the strongest available evidence that sourcehood was never going to be found in self-origination.

## 6. Pereboom's Four Cases and the Structure of Bypass

Pereboom's four-case argument (*Living Without Free Will*, 2001; high confidence) is the sharpest blade in the hard incompatibilist armory. It works as follows. Case 1: Professor Plum has been created moments ago by neuroscientists who manipulate him in real time to reason and act egoistically, issuing in a murder. He meets every standard compatibilist condition — reasons-responsiveness, second-order endorsement, guidance control — yet is intuitively not responsible. He is a puppet, regardless of whether his internal states meet the compatibilist criteria. Cases 2, 3, and 4 progressively remove the external manipulators: prior programming, then deterministic upbringing engineered to produce these dispositions, then ordinary determinism. Each case, Pereboom argues, is relevantly like the last. If Case 1 produces no responsibility and Case 4 is relevantly similar, no determined agent is responsible.

The reply here rests on the arity argument. The break between Case 1 and Case 4 is not a difference in local dynamics — Pereboom is right that those can be matched — and not the mere presence of visible manipulators. The difference is whether the agent's sourcehood was *constituted through the second-personal community* or *bypassed it*. Manipulation, in the morally relevant sense, is shaping that bypasses the address: the neuroscientists of Case 1 operate *on* Plum in the first-to-third mode — they do not address him, demand of him, stand in mutual accountability with him such that his uptake of their address is a success condition for anything. Ordinary formation runs *through* the second-personal: a child is addressed, held responsible for the quality of its will toward others, drawn into the reactive-attitude community, takes up principles as its own *in dialogue with other sources* (Fischer and Ravizza, *Responsibility and Control*, 1998; high confidence on this mechanism).

The opponent will press: "through the address-community" versus "by bypass" is not a kind-difference but a place on a gradient — a difference in causal route, and every transition from Case 1 to Case 4 differs in causal route. This objection is the sharpest available, and it has a precise answer. A smooth variation in *degree* of second-personal constitution is fully consistent with a categorical difference at the limit. Responsibility is *graded* exactly because second-personal constitution is graded — cults and coercive control are hard precisely because the address-community is *partially* bypassed. The theory predicts this, rather than being embarrassed by it. What the opponent needs is not "the cases shade into each other" (granted) but "there is no point at which second-personal constitution reaches zero while the dynamics stay matched." There is such a point: Case 1, real-time external authorship, where the address-community's contribution to *this act's* sourcehood is nil. A continuous function can still take the value zero. A dimmer switch is continuous and still has an off.

The criterion sorting the cases does not beg the question. It is the arity argument, fixed independently: *taking-up* is the relation in which the agent occupies the second pole of an address — has standing to answer and refuse, whose uptake is part of the act's success conditions — whereas *installing* is the first-to-third operation in which the agent's uptake is not a success condition. This distinction is attested in linguistics, developmental psychology, and the philosophy of indexicals *before* any of the parties reach the free will question. Apply it to ordinary child development: a child who does not take up the address is not formed, only conditioned, and we mark exactly that difference in ordinary discourse — we distinguish raising a child from training a dog *by* the second-personal uptake condition. Apply it to Case 1: the manipulators get the murder whether or not Plum answers anything. That asymmetry — whether uptake is a success condition — is the criterion, and it is not derived from the conclusion it is being used to sort.

## 7. Compatibilism, the Regress, and What Conferred Sourcehood Adds

Standard compatibilism — Frankfurt's hierarchical account (1971, *Journal of Philosophy*; high confidence) and Fischer and Ravizza's guidance control — holds that an act is free when it flows from the self in the right way: first-order desire endorsed by second-order volition, reasons-responsive mechanism, owned. This is genuine progress. The Frankfurt cases, whatever their dialectical status against the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, correctly locate the question in the *actual sequence* rather than in counterfactual alternatives.

The debt ordinary compatibilism cannot discharge is what Watson and Velleman (cited in substance as this critique's source; high confidence on attribution of the regress pressure) press as the regress of identification: why is the second-order volition *authoritative*? Because the agent identifies with it. And the third? At some point the identification must simply settle, and that settling looks like a small aseity — a point where the buck stops *in* the self as an unexplained identifier. Ordinary compatibilism tends to install an authoritative self to halt the regress and not pay for it in the currency of an account.

Conferred sourcehood diverges here, and the divergence is its whole substantive contribution to the compatibilism literature. It does not halt the regress in the self. It denies that the regress needs a self-standing terminus at all. The buck does not stop *in* the agent as an uncaused identifier; it stops at a standpoint that is itself conferred — caused, constituted, re-enacted — whose authority is bestowed-and-inhabited, not self-generated. Christine Korsgaard (*Self-Constitution*, 2009; high confidence) captures the structural point: agency is the continuous activity of constituting oneself as author, not a standing property of a pre-existing self. The "self" is not the fixed point from which the endorsement comes; it *is* the endorsing activity, re-enacted each time, grounded in nothing more self-standing than anything else in a no-aseity world. This makes conferred sourcehood not a version of compatibilism but an account that is compatible with compatibilism's best insights while declining its deepest evasion.

## 8. Forgiveness, Resentment, and the Evidence of the Second Person

Two further phenomena illuminate the arity argument from different angles.

**Forgiveness.** The hard incompatibilist can model forgiveness as the disposition-reset that restores cooperative expected value after a defection — the maintenance of resentment is costly, its termination signals that the cooperation channel is re-open. This is a real and non-trivial functional story. But observe what it leaves unspecified: it predicts the behavioral and signaling profile of "I have forgiven you" without capturing the directed character of forgiveness as an act. I can reset my dispositions toward you, re-open the cooperation channel, and halt the resentment-maintenance loop *without forgiving you* — by simply ceasing to care, or by writing you off as a hazard now contained, or by a unilateral prudential recalculation in which you are never addressed at all. The behavioral and signaling profile of "I have forgiven you" and "I have written you off as no longer worth resenting" can be made identical. What differs is whether a *claim against a source was released to that source*. Forgiveness has an addressee who can *refuse* it; dropping a maintenance cost has no addressee to refuse anything. The asymmetry is exhibited, not asserted: "I forgive you" requires a "you" who could say "I do not accept your forgiveness" in response, and that response has normative consequences (it keeps the claim alive, it registers a standing grievance) that "I have ceased to maintain the resentment-loop" has no analog for. The second-personal structure is visible in the grammar of the practice.

**The objective stance as status-demotion.** P.F. Strawson noted that being moved to the objective attitude toward a person — treating them as a system to be managed rather than a source to be addressed — feels like a demotion in their status. The hard incompatibilist can explain why it *feels bad*: on the shaping account, being moved from the participant protocol to the management protocol is a fitness-relevant status-demotion for a social primate, so of course it registers as loss. This is a genuinely strong predictive match. But observe that it predicts the *valence* (it feels bad) without predicting the *content* (what the hurt is about). The shaping account predicts: "moved to management protocol → loss → feels bad." It does not predict that the loss will present as a withdrawal of *standing to answer* — as "you are treating me as something that cannot answer you" rather than "you have lowered my expected cooperative value." These are different intentional contents, and the second is the one actually reported by people moved to the objective stance: the patient who says "stop managing me and *talk to me*"; the prisoner whose grievance is not "you have predicted my behavior" but "you treat me as if I cannot answer." The second-personal account predicts that specific content independently of the free will question. The shaping account predicts only the valence and must treat the content as confabulation. That is the remainder, and it is independently attested — in linguistics, in the philosophy of indexicals, in developmental psychology's account of the second person — before anyone reaches the question of free will.

## 9. The Occasionalism of Agency: A Narrowed Claim

The corpus from which this paper is drawn develops a companion claim — the "occasionalism of agency" — which holds that authorship drains and returns in ways that do not always track a dynamical difference. That claim, as originally stated, is withdrawn. Under the framework's own physicalism, every real psychological transition — the addict watching herself relapse, the dissociated subject watching herself act — is a real dynamical transition. Frankfurt's unwilling addict (1971; high confidence), the person moved by a will she does not endorse, is in a different dynamical state from the integrated agent. IFS's part-led/Self-led distinction, if it tracks something real, tracks a real network-configuration difference. The original claim that agency-loss does not always track a dynamical difference is false, and the falsity is conceded.

What survives is a narrower proposition: ownership of an act is not fixed by, and does not reduce to, the agent's *local synchronic dynamical state*. Two episodes can be matched in the agent's instantaneous dynamical profile and differ in authorship, because authorship is constituted by relational-historical physical facts — specifically, whether the mechanism was taken up through the second-personal address-community or installed by bypass — and those facts do not show up in the instantaneous state. Ownership supervenes on the total physical facts (no dualism) but not on the local dynamics (no reduction-to-dynamics). This is a weaker claim and it is stated as the weaker claim. Its sole clean witness is the manipulation case, which means it depends on the arity argument rather than floating on independent evidence. It is stated at moderate confidence, as dependent on the second-personal remainder rather than corroborating it.

The concrete significance of this narrowed claim: Pereboom's four-case argument holds the structural-dispositional conditions fixed and varies etiology. It cannot, on its own terms, simultaneously hold fixed the *second-personal standing*, because second-personal standing is not a structural-dispositional fact. That is the gap the arity argument opens; the narrowed occasionalism argument is the observation that the gap is filled by relational-historical physical facts, not by anything supernatural.

## 10. Conferred Sourcehood: The Verdict

The argument of this paper may be assembled as a single structure.

The aseity premise — the demand that free will consist in self-origination — is a smuggled assumption, not an analytic necessity. Dropping it does not leave a vacuum. It leaves *conferred sourcehood*: the standpoint that is caused, constituted, addressed, and re-enacted; the authorship that is inhabited rather than generated; the self that is, as Korsgaard has it, the continuous activity of constituting an author rather than a pre-existing object whose endorsements are read off.

Conferred sourcehood is not a deflationary position. It does not lower the bar for what responsibility requires; it challenges whether the bar was ever correctly described. The three cross-traditional witnesses show that ownership-without-origination is not an evasion but a robust independently-arrived-at structure. The arity argument shows that the hard incompatibilist's proposed replacement of the reactive attitudes with forward-looking management is not a reduction but an elimination — an elimination of the second argument-place of a two-place relation, which cannot be accomplished by supplying a one-place predicate however finely calibrated.

The proposal, stated exactly: holding-responsible is a two-place relation whose second relatum is an addressee *qua addressee* — a source who can answer, against whom a claim can lie, whose refusal of forgiveness keeps the claim alive, whose uptake of address is part of the success conditions of address. No third-personal, forward-looking, one-place description of behavior-shaping captures that second relatum. The eliminativist owes such a description. Structurally, it cannot be supplied, because the second argument-place is not a predicate to be stripped but a relatum to be either included or discarded. Discard it and you have not refined holding-responsible; you have abolished it and produced something else — something useful, perhaps, but not the same thing.

Determinism is granted in full throughout. The consequence argument is granted. The Basic Argument is granted through premise 5. What the determinism and the Basic Argument together establish is that the agent is not the ultimate, self-originating source of anything. That is true and important. What they do not establish — what they cannot establish without the aseity premise smuggled back in at the conclusion — is that there is therefore no sourcehood, no responsibility, no second-personal standing. Conferred sourcehood claims none of the things the hard incompatibilist successfully refutes. It claims only what the arity argument shows cannot be refuted by any one-place vocabulary: the irreducible two-place structure of an address, the remainder that forgiveness exhibits and that the solipsism test proves does not reduce to the necessity of a locus.

**Verdict on the argument.** The arity gap — the structural impossibility of target-fixing a two-place relation with a one-place vocabulary — passes the test for substantive independence. It is falsifiable: the argument fails if a complete forward-looking redescription is supplied that captures, without remainder, the second-personal differentials — forgiving-while-deterring versus deterring-without-forgiving, releasing-a-claim versus dropping-a-cost, being-owed-an-account versus predicting-behavior. No such description has been supplied. The eliminativist's bullet is unbitten, not proven unbiteable. That distinction is maintained throughout and it is the honest bound.

The argument would be insincere if it exempted the page it is printed on. Authority, like sourcehood, cannot be self-bestowed. A body of work that declares itself canonical by self-assertion inhabits the same aseity error the argument was written to expose. Authority is conferred and relational; it is constituted, like sourcehood, in a second-personal community that can answer back and withhold uptake. The argument applies reflexively or it is not serious.
